The Star That Erupts Every 113 Days Has a Hidden Thief

A giant star 30 million light-years away has flared at least 27 times since 2012, on a strict schedule. NASA's Chandra telescope finally caught why: an unseen black hole or neutron star is stealing its gas, orbit after orbit.

By Juno·Tuesday, November 10, 2026·Edition 56

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Some stars explode once. AT 2016blu erupts on a schedule. The giant star, in the spiral galaxy NGC 4559 about 30 million light-years from Earth, has produced at least 27 bursts of visible light between 2012 and 2026 — roughly one every 113 days. On November 9, NASA's Chandra X-ray Observatory announced what is setting them off. [1][2]

The answer is a companion no one can see. When Chandra looked during an outburst in March 2026, the system was at least 100 times brighter in X-rays than it had been in observations from 2001 and 2002, before the eruptions began. The team's explanation, published in The Astrophysical Journal, is that a hidden, extremely dense companion — either a stellar-mass black hole or a neutron star, the collapsed core of a dead massive star — orbits the giant every 113 days and pulls material off it with each pass, and the stolen gas flares as it falls in. [1]

AT 2016blu is a luminous blue variable, a hot, massive star prone to large changes in brightness. What makes this one different is the proof: this is the first X-ray detection of this class of erupting star, and the first of these objects found with a black hole or neutron star companion. Scientists have not directly photographed the companion or conclusively identified which of the two it is — the X-rays are the fingerprint, not a portrait. [1][2]

That distinction is the whole story. For fourteen years, astronomers watched a star flare like clockwork and argued about why. One X-ray observation during a single outburst turned a mystery into a mechanism: a thief in orbit, stealing a little gas every 113 days, and lighting up the crime scene each time it does.
