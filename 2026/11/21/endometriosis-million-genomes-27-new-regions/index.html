One in Ten Women Live With It. A Million-Person Study Just Found 27 New Places in the Genome Tied to It.

An NIH-backed study pooling 14 biobanks found 58 genetic regions linked to endometriosis, shared genetic ties to 135 other conditions, and 314 genes worth a closer look.

By Publius·Saturday, November 21, 2026·Edition 67

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Endometriosis is common, painful and poorly understood. Tissue similar to the lining of the uterus grows where it doesn't belong, causing severe pain and fertility problems, and it affects up to one in ten women worldwide, according to the National Institutes of Health. [1] Patients often wait years for a diagnosis, and the treatments mostly manage symptoms. A study published Friday in Nature Genetics doesn't change that yet, but it gives researchers a much bigger map. [1][2]

The team pooled genetic data from 14 biobanks around the world, covering more than 1 million people. [1] More than 30% of them were of non-European ancestry, NIH said, which matters because earlier genome-wide studies of the disease drew mostly on people of European descent, with some smaller East Asian samples. [1]

The results, as described by NIH: [1]

  • 58 regions of the genome linked to endometriosis, 27 of them never reported before.
  • New genetic links found specifically by studying people of African and Admixed-American ancestry, groups with mixed North, Central and South American heritage.
  • Shared genetic links between endometriosis and 135 other health conditions or traits.
  • 314 genes flagged as possibly involved, found through "multi-omic" analyses that layer several kinds of biological data on top of the DNA.

The researchers also cast a wide net on who counted as a case, using a broad range of endometriosis diagnoses rather than one narrow definition. [1]

"By studying many types of biological data across people with different ancestries, we were able to increase our understanding of how endometriosis develops and affects the body," said senior author Shefali Setia-Verma of the University of Pennsylvania's Perelman School of Medicine. [1] She said the findings could "eventually lead to better diagnosis, personalized risk assessment, and treatments that target the disease." [1]

It's worth being plain about what a study like this does and doesn't do. A genome-wide association study finds stretches of DNA that show up more often in people with a disease. It doesn't prove what those stretches do, and a flagged gene isn't a drug target until someone shows how it works. NIH's own release says the findings "could someday lead to studies on potential targets" for treatment, which is careful language for a reason. [1] The 135 shared conditions are genetic overlaps, not proof that endometriosis causes them.

The study was supported by NIH's Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, whose director, John Gaitanis, said NIH is "actively pursuing a cure." [1] The paper is Guare and colleagues, "Multi-ancestry genome-wide association and integrative multi-omics analyses implicate key pathways in endometriosis." [1][2]

Commentary. This is the kind of research the public can see the point of: pooled data, a big and varied sample, results published openly, and modest claims about what comes next. It's also a reminder that the work runs through a federal agency whose funding sits inside the same December 11 deadline as everything else in Washington.
