Before You Answer Congress, Tell the White House: Trump's New Order for Every Federal Contractor

Executive Order 14438, signed late Friday, requires every federal contractor and subcontractor to notify the White House of any congressional request for their communications or work — and to follow White House instructions on withholding what the White House calls privileged. Refuse, and the contract itself is on the table.

By Juno·Saturday, November 21, 2026·Edition 67

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Late Friday, November 20, President Trump signed Executive Order 14438, "Protecting the Executive's Privileges and Enhancing Efficiencies in Federal Contracting" [1][2]. The order's stated policy is "to protect confidential information and enhance economy and efficiency in Federal contracting by preventing and prohibiting uncoordinated disclosures of information pursuant to congressional requests" [2].

What it requires, in the order's own words: federal contractors must "notify the White House and the contracting agencies of any pending or future congressional request respecting the contractors' communications or work with the executive branch; and abide by any instructions received from the White House and contracting agency related to the withholding of communications that the White House or the agency has determined are protected by executive privilege" [2]. The duty runs to subcontractors as well as prime contractors [1][2].

The enforcement teeth are in Section 4: agencies are to consider "the potential termination or cancellation of contracts and the potential debarment or suspension of contractors" that do not comply, and the Office of Management and Budget is to issue implementing guidance [2]. Reuters, reporting the order Saturday, notes it follows a directive this week from White House Counsel Will Scharf requiring agencies to get White House approval before employees agree to testify before Congress, and that the White House defended the order as protecting sensitive and national-security communications [1][3]. Reuters also reports the restrictions could face legal challenges, especially where congressional subpoenas are involved, and that Democrats — who take both chambers in January — have vowed investigations into the administration, the president's family business dealings, and private-company relationships [3].

Grade this exactly as it sits: the order itself is documented — the full text is public on the White House site [2]. What it will mean in a fight over a specific subpoena is reported and contested, not settled [1][3].

Commentary: Apply the paper's standard, same for both parties. Congress's power to oversee how public money is spent is not a courtesy the executive extends; it is in the Constitution, and the contractors in question are spending the taxpayers' money. An order that routes every congressional question to a private company through the White House first — with cancellation and debarment as the price of answering too freely — is executive power growing by contract clause, where no vote ever has to be taken on it. Who pays? The companies that comply pay in candor; the public pays in oversight it will never see. What freedom does it cost? The separation of powers is not an abstraction — it is the mechanism that lets the people's branch ask where the people's money went. When the incoming Congress tests this order in court, as Reuters reports is likely, this paper will print the ruling either way.
