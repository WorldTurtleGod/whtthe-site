Mississippi Just Sent Its First Black Senator to Washington Since Reconstruction

Scott Colom beat a Republican incumbent in the state both parties had written off — on the price of beef, not the culture war.

By Juno·Friday, November 13, 2026·Edition 59

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Mississippi has not elected a Democrat to the U.S. Senate since 1982. It has not sent a Black senator to Washington since Reconstruction. Both of those streaks ended Wednesday.

The Associated Press called the race for Democrat Scott Colom on Nov. 11, more than a week after Election Day, once absentee and provisional ballots were counted. Colom, a district attorney, beat Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith by roughly 4,600 votes — 49.2 percent to 48.7 percent — the closest Senate race in the country. [1][2]

Hyde-Smith conceded in a video posted to social media Wednesday night, after calling Colom to congratulate him. "While this is not the outcome we worked for or hoped for, I respect the decision of Mississippi voters," she said. "I want to congratulate Scott Colom and wish him well as he takes on the tremendous responsibility of representing our great state." [1]

Colom gave his victory speech at Tougaloo College, the historically Black college in Jackson, opening with a tribute to Hiram Revels, Mississippi's first Black U.S. senator. [2] He told supporters the win belonged to the people who knocked on the doors, not to him: "For months and months, I've been telling everybody everywhere I go that we're gonna win this election. But every time I said it, I pointed out that the people were gonna give me too much credit for it. Because it's not something that I did, y'all. It's something that we did!" [1]

The result is the biggest upset of the midterms, and the way it happened matters as much as the fact of it. Mississippi has the highest share of Black voters in the country, close to 40 percent, but Trump won the state by 23 points in 2024, and the Cook Political Report rated the race Likely Republican going into election night. National Democrats spent almost nothing in Mississippi until the final days. Colom said so himself: "The reality is, Mississippi has never been a lost cause, we've been a betrayed cause. The Democratic Party has not been trying to win Mississippi." [1]

He did not win it on the national party's message. He won it on the price of groceries. Colom made the cost of living the centerpiece of his campaign, branding his opponent "Cindy High-Prices," and when Hyde-Smith — a cattle rancher and former state agriculture commissioner — was asked about high beef prices and answered that "you have so many other proteins to choose from," Colom turned it into a viral ad: time to "pick another senator." [1]

The win expands the Democratic majority in the new Senate to 55 seats, with Alaska still undecided. [1] That number matters for January. But the number that matters for Mississippi is the one on the map: a state that had not been competitive in a generation just proved it was never as settled as everyone assumed. The parties wrote Mississippi off. The voters did not write themselves off.
