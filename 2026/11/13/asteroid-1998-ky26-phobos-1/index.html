The Asteroid That Won't Obey Gravity — and the Lost Soviet Probe It Might Be

A 36-foot rock called 1998 KY26 is accelerating in a way gravity can't explain. One new study rules out the comet theory. Another team says it might be Phobos 1, lost since 1988.

By Juno·Friday, November 13, 2026·Edition 59

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Something is pushing a 36-foot rock through space, and gravity is not the explanation.

The object is 1998 KY26, a near-Earth asteroid about 36 feet wide that spins once every 5.35 minutes and reflects roughly 52 percent of the sunlight that hits it. Astronomers call its odd movement "nongravitational acceleration" — something besides the pull of the Sun and the planets is affecting its path. [1]

The leading explanation was that KY26 is a "dark comet," a rock with buried water ice that turns to gas and gives it a push, even though no cloud or tail has ever been seen. A new peer-reviewed study by Georgia Tech researchers Korbin Hansen and Masatoshi Hirabayashi, published in the journal Icarus, casts doubt on that: KY26 is likely too warm for buried water ice to survive long enough to account for the movement. Their conclusion: calculated water-loss rates "preclude 1998 KY26 being a dark comet at present." [1]

With the comet theory wobbling, another group has explored a far stranger possibility. Harvard astrophysicist Avi Loeb, with researchers Adam Hibberd and Adam Crowl, modeled whether KY26 could actually be Phobos 1, the unmanned Soviet spacecraft that stopped communicating while traveling to Mars in 1988. They built a simplified model of Phobos 1 and compared how it would reflect sunlight as it spun with observations of KY26 — and found the model closely matched eight sets of observations. They also found that sunlight pushing against a cylinder or solar panels could help explain the object's movement better than the asteroid models. [1]

The researchers are careful about what this proves, which is not much yet: "Although an investigation of this kind cannot be conclusive," they wrote, the findings "add strong weight to the Phobos 1 hypothesis." They call it "strongly plausible but presently unproven." [1]

NASA is not panicking. Administrator Jared Isaacman weighed in on Fox & Friends on Thursday morning, Nov. 12: "First of all, nothing is about to hit us, to be very clear on this. I've not seen any evidence to suggest this is a threat to our planet or anything other than an asteroid." [1] Other research points the same way — a separate study published this year in Astronomy & Astrophysics examined KY26's reflected light and found it consistent with a rocky asteroid from the inner asteroid belt. [1]

So the sober money is still on a rock. But the fun of this story is that we will actually find out. The Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency's Hayabusa2 spacecraft is scheduled to rendezvous with KY26 in July 2031, and Loeb has already placed his bet: "Our prediction is that instead of an iceberg, Hayabusa 2's cameras will reveal the label 'Made in the U.S.S.R.'" [1] Five years from now, a camera either photographs a rock or a Soviet spacecraft. Either way, the sky wins.
