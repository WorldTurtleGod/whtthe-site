Physicists Found a New Knob on the "Magic" Material: Just Stretch It

University of Washington researchers squeezed and stretched twisted sheets of carbon one atom thick, and the material's electrical resistance swung more than 100 times harder than an ordinary metal's would.

By Herodotus·Friday, November 13, 2026·Edition 59

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Some of the strangest physics of the past decade comes from a trick a child could describe: take two sheets of something, lay one on top of the other, and twist.

The sheets in question are graphene, carbon just one atom thick. When two of them are stacked and rotated against each other, the honeycomb lattices form a shifting moiré pattern, like two window screens held up together. [1] In 2018, MIT researchers showed that at a "magic angle" of 1.1 degrees, cooled to near absolute zero, the stack can become a superconductor, carrying electricity with no resistance at all. [1] Since then, labs around the world have coaxed new behavior out of these materials by changing temperature, voltage and magnetic fields. [1]

Now a team at the University of Washington has added one more dial: strain. In a paper published Nov. 12 in Science, they showed that stretching and compressing the material produces big swings in its electrical resistance. [1]

The idea grew out of a nuisance. Getting two atom-thin flakes to lie perfectly on top of each other is nearly impossible, so the sheets often warp and stretch, muddying the pattern. [1] "It's like saying, 'I'm going to give you two massive sheets of shrink wrap and demand that you place them perfectly flat without any wrinkles,'" said senior author Matthew Yankowitz, an associate professor of physics and of materials science and engineering. [1]

Instead of fighting the warping, Yankowitz and physics professor Jiun-Haw Chu decided to control it. They mounted the flakes on a device that expands and contracts when voltage is applied, which let them tune the strain smoothly. [1] Adjusting the strain changed the material's resistance more than 100 times as much as it would in an ordinary metal, and the effect grew stronger at very low temperatures. [1]

"We've now added a fundamentally new way to manipulate them, by directly deforming the atomic lattice they live on," Yankowitz said. "That enables experiments we simply couldn't do before." [1]

The more tantalizing part is the company the result keeps. "What is remarkable about this giant response to strain is that it also appears in other exotic materials, including high-temperature superconductors, one of the great unsolved problems in physics," Chu said. [1] The team next wants to simplify the setup so other labs can repeat it and try it on other atom-thin materials. [1]

A small echo. A great deal of useful science began as people poking at something strange long before anyone knew what it was good for. This is that kind of work: a new way to ask a material questions. Superconducting power lines and quantum computers may or may not come out of it, but the asking is the point.
