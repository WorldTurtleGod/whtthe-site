Euclid Just Mapped 900 Million Galaxies

ESA's Euclid telescope released its first big batch of data: more than a billion objects, the foundation of the largest 3D map of the universe ever attempted.

By Juno·Friday, November 13, 2026·Edition 59

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

The European Space Agency and the Euclid Consortium released the first major batch of data from the Euclid space telescope on Nov. 12 — and the numbers are almost hard to hold in your head. The release, called DR1 Foundation, captures light from more than a billion objects and covers an area of sky equal to 9,500 full moons. [1]

Inside it: more than 900 million galaxies. Around 15,000 strong gravitational lens candidates — more than all previous catalogs combined. More than 60 million quasar candidates. Spectra from more than 160 million sources. The whole thing is 1.7 million gigabytes of data, covering 1,900 square degrees of sky — about 14 percent of what Euclid will survey over its six-year mission. [1]

The data comes from about 13 months of observations, the first major release since Euclid launched in mid-2023. For the first time, scientists have the pieces needed to start building a detailed 3D map of the cosmos — the map Euclid was built to make, covering the last 10 billion years of the universe, to chase down dark energy and dark matter and the cosmic web that holds galaxies together. [1]

"These are far more than beautiful pictures; today marks a scientific milestone in our study of the cosmos," said Carole Mundell, ESA's director of science. "This is a change in scale from Euclid's previous releases, which included a few days of observations over around 60 deg² of sky," said Valeria Pettorino, Euclid project scientist. "We're now releasing more than a year of data covering a significantly larger area. This unlocks groundbreaking science." [1]

A note of proportion: this release is raw material, not answers. It is a catalog, not a measurement of dark energy. The full Data Release 1, due in mid-2027, will add weak-lensing analysis and more of the pieces needed to actually weigh the dark universe. What Nov. 12 gave astronomers is the largest space-based image dataset ever produced — 2,500 researchers at more than 300 institutions are now mining it. [1]

Nine hundred million galaxies, and that is 14 percent of the survey. The map Euclid is building will eventually hold the positions of billions of galaxies across ten billion years of cosmic history. We spent centuries arguing about whether the universe had an edge. Now we are counting its contents, a billion at a time.
