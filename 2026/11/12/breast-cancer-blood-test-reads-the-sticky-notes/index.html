A Blood Test That Reads Cancer's Sticky Notes

Israeli researchers report in Nature that an experimental test, reading epigenetic tags on DNA fragments in the blood, picked out early breast cancer with 95% accuracy and helped predict whose cancer would come back. It is still a small study, not a screening tool.

By Herodotus·Thursday, November 12, 2026·Edition 58

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Every cell in your body carries the same cookbook. What makes a liver cell a liver cell is the set of sticky notes telling it which recipes to use. That is how the Weizmann Institute of Science explains epigenetics, and in cancer, the sticky notes go wrong as well as the recipes. [1]

A team led by Prof. Efrat Shema of Weizmann and Prof. Guy Ron of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem says it can now read those notes from a blood sample well enough to spot early breast cancer. Their study was published Wednesday, Nov. 11, in Nature. [1][2][4]

The trick is in what dying cells leave behind. When a cell dies it sheds nucleosomes, short lengths of DNA wound around proteins, into the bloodstream, and cancer cells do too. [1] Shema's lab had built a technology that uses single-molecule microscopy and fluorescent labels to map dozens of different epigenetic tags across millions of those nucleosomes. A 2022 study had already shown promise for colorectal and pancreatic cancers. [1] Ron's physics group treated the flood of signals as an experimental physics problem and hunted for the patterns. [1]

First, they needed a map. The team recruited 12 women scheduled for tumor surgery. "Once a tumor is removed, there's only a short window of opportunity before its epigenetic tags begin to deteriorate," said Dr. Noa Furth of Shema's lab. "I would rush the tissue sample from the hospital to our lab on ice, and we'd start analyzing it that very day." [1]

Then came the test. On blood samples from 77 breast cancer patients followed for at least five years at the Mayo Clinic in Florida, plus 30 healthy women, AI models trained on the epigenetic readings told the groups apart with 95% accuracy for early-stage cancer: tumors still confined to the breast and no larger than two centimeters. [1][3] That figure is a statistical score called AUC, a measure of how well the model separates the two groups. It is not a promise that the test would catch 95 out of 100 cancers in the general public. [4] Shema said the test worked regardless of breast density, which matters because dense tissue lets tumors "hide" on mammograms. She said it also held up across cancer types and in an expanded set of 188 patient blood samples from Sheba and Kaplan medical centers. [1]

The second finding may matter more. Breast cancer returns in 20% to 40% of cases even after early treatment that seems to work. [1] The researchers found that even tumors not considered aggressive can harbor a rare population of highly aggressive, invasive cells with their own epigenetic signature. The paper describes them as basal-like cells inside luminal tumors. [1][2] "The differences appear to be mainly epigenetic," Shema said. [1] The more of that signature turned up in a patient's blood, the higher the risk of relapse. Combined with other markers, it separated patients whose cancer came back from those whose didn't with 81% accuracy, and it flagged risk in some patients that conventional measures rated low. [1][3]

A word of caution, plainly put. The largest set of blood samples in the study was 188, not tens of thousands, and the test is still experimental. [1][4] As JBizNews put it, the results are not evidence that the test can replace mammograms or is ready for routine screening. [4] Results like these have to hold up in much larger groups of patients first.
