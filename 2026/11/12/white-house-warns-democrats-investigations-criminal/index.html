The White House Just Told Congress That Investigating It Could Be a Crime

The counsel's letter to the incoming Oversight chairman reads like a warning shot at Article I itself — and the Democrats answered with two words.

By Juno·Thursday, November 12, 2026·Edition 58

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

The White House counsel has put Congress on notice: investigate us, and you might be committing a crime.

William Scharf, the White House counsel, sent a letter dated Monday, Nov. 9, to Rep. Robert Garcia of California, the incoming chairman of the House Oversight Committee, warning that the Democrats' planned investigations "may cross the line into blatant criminality." The letter was first reported by Punchbowl News and only became public on Nov. 11. [1][2]

Scharf's argument is that congressional investigations have to "further a legitimate legislative task," not punish targets — and he cites Supreme Court precedent, Watkins v. United States and Trump v. Mazars USA, for the proposition. [1] He pointed to Democrats' own post-election boasting: Rep. Emily Randall saying subpoenas would be handed out "like candy," Rep. Analilia Mejia saying they'd be tossed out "like Mardi Gras beads," and Garcia's election-night post on X: "Oversight Democrats are ready to take on the crooks, the con men, and the Trump crime family. Lawyer up." [1][3]

Then the threat, in Scharf's own words: "Issuing unlawful subpoenas with the intention of depriving citizens of their constitutional rights and privileges may warrant further investigation." And: "The Administration will not be extorted and will not be cowed by unlawful process motivated by animus, pettiness, hatred, or any other unlawful motive." [1]

Garcia answered the same day, in a statement posted on the House Oversight Democrats' X page: "It appears Donald Trump has something to hide." And the constitutional counterpunch: "Let's be clear: Congress' authority comes from Article I of the Constitution, not from permission of the White House." He added that Democrats "will not be deterred by baseless threats from a weaponized DOJ or from the President's lawyers." [1][2]

The stakes here are not theoretical. Democrats retook both chambers in the Nov. 3 midterms and take control in January. The probe targets named in the reporting: Elon Musk, the Trump family's crypto fortune, Trump construction projects, the Iran war, and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth. [1] This follows Attorney General Todd Blanche saying on CNN on Nov. 8 that prosecuting Democratic lawmakers for overzealous oversight threats was possible. [3]

Step back and look at what Scharf is actually claiming. Congress's power to investigate is not a courtesy the executive branch extends; it is the power the Constitution gives the legislature to find out what the government is doing with the people's money and power. The Supreme Court cases Scharf cites are real — Watkins and Mazars do put limits on congressional inquiries — but neither case says the White House gets to pre-clear subpoenas, and neither turns a subpoena the president dislikes into a crime. A letter that says "investigate us and we may investigate you" is not legal analysis. It is a warning shot, and it was fired before a single subpoena has been issued.

The irony is thick enough to cut. This is the same administration whose allies spent years treating "lawyer up" as a punchline and congressional subpoenas as a lifestyle. Now that the gavels are about to change hands, the same words are suddenly "blatant criminality." [3] The Democrats, for their part, are not helping their own case by bragging about handing out subpoenas like parade candy — Randall's and Mejia's quotes are the kind of thing that makes Scharf's letter sound almost reasonable to a casual reader. Oversight is a constitutional duty, not a victory lap, and the moment it becomes a victory lap it hands the other side the argument.

What happens next is a standoff, not a lawsuit. Garcia's committee had not even formally received the letter as of Wednesday morning, according to a spokesperson. [1] When the new Congress is seated in January, the Democrats will either issue the subpoenas they promised or they won't. If they do, the White House will either fight them in court — where Mazars actually governs, and where the administration would have to argue each subpoena on its merits — or it will try to make good on Scharf's threat. One of those paths is constitutional government. The other is a president telling the legislature that looking into him is a crime. Watch which one they pick.
