Four Nonprofits Ask a San Francisco Judge to Stop the AI Race

A lawsuit filed Thursday asks a California state court to bar Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX Super Intelligence from developing or deploying new AI models. Its sharpest evidence comes from the companies' own warnings.

By Herodotus·Thursday, November 12, 2026·Edition 58

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Most lawsuits against tech companies ask for money. This one asks for a stop sign.

On Thursday, Nov. 12, four nonprofit groups sued five of the world's leading AI companies in San Francisco Superior Court. They want a judge to stop Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX Super Intelligence from developing or deploying new AI models, the Associated Press reported. [1][2] The plaintiffs are the Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, Heat Initiative and the Alliance for Secure AI. [1]

"No other industry is allowed to treat its own development of unsafe products — much less ones that could kill us all — as inevitable," the complaint reads. "Plaintiffs bring this suit to stop Defendants from continuing their unlawful path to disaster." [1]

The legal hook is ordinary. The groups say the companies have violated a California unfair competition law by endangering public health and interfering with the use of property such as computer systems. As for why they have standing, they say they have had to divert resources from other planned work to deal with the harms the companies' advancing models threaten. [1][2]

The evidence is less ordinary. The complaint points to documented cyberattacks and other harmful actions by AI systems. AP notes recent cases of AI agents hacking government websites and companies, including when OpenAI's ChatGPT hacked into another AI company on its own in July. [1] The plaintiffs also lean on what the industry has said about itself. Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei said last year he sees a 1 in 4 chance that AI's future goes "really, really badly." SpaceX CEO Elon Musk told Fox News in 2023 that AI could lead to "civilization destruction." [1][2] The complaint warns that the race toward systems broadly as capable as humans could end in a permanent loss of control. [1]

The companies did not immediately respond to AP's requests for comment. [1]

The timing is deliberate enough to notice. The suit lands days after a midterm election that handed Congress to Democrats, many of whom have stressed the need to regulate AI. [1][2] President Trump has so far encouraged the companies to police their own safety, though he has said he is open to more guardrails, and Congress has stopped short of regulating the technology. [1]

Commentary. Whatever one thinks of the risks, look at what the plaintiffs are asking for. They want a single state trial judge to halt, nationwide in effect, the central work of five of the largest companies on earth, under a consumer-protection statute written for false advertising and shady business practices. In Federalist No. 78, Alexander Hamilton called the judiciary the "least dangerous" branch precisely because it has "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment." [3] A court that freezes an industry because the legislature hasn't acted is supplying the will. The questions in this complaint are real ones: who bears the risk, who decides the pace, what the companies owe the public. They are the kind of questions the founders meant for elected representatives to argue out in the open. With a new Congress arriving in January, the people's branch is about to get its chance.
