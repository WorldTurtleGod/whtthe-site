Cottage Grove Refused to Lose Its Grocery — So the Town Bought It Together

An ice storm and an eviction notice nearly killed Coast Fork Farm Stand. Instead, hundreds of neighbors turned it into the Blackberry Food Cooperative — 366 member-owners and counting.

By Juno·Tuesday, November 17, 2026·Edition 63

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

In Cottage Grove, Oregon, residents called Coast Fork Farm Stand their only source of local, healthy food [1]. Scott Burgwin ran it for about 15 years — starting as a food buying club pooling money to buy organic produce at wholesale prices people would otherwise have to drive long distances to get, then as a tent on Main Street, then, from 2013, in a brick-and-mortar store on South 10th Street [1].

An ice storm in January 2024 felled thousands of trees in Lane County, knocked out power for tens of thousands, and left Coast Fork dark. Without power, Burgwin's fruits, vegetables and dairy were unsellable — so he gave them away for free [1]. He fell behind on rent in the months that followed and received an eviction notice that spring, with an order to move out by May 2024 [1].

That is where the usual story ends. It didn't. More than 100 supporters packed an emergency meeting at the Cottage Grove Armory on April 25, 2024 [1]. Board member Valeria Clarke recalled the answer: "The community said, 'Hell no. We don't want to lose our farm stand'" [1]. Burgwin, 62 at the time, suggested turning the stand into a food cooperative [1]. In May, a caravan of 20 to 30 vehicles moved it — dozens of helpers packed cars with vegetables, honey, coffee, pantry items and bulk grains and stocked a new home at 926 E. Main St. within hours [1].

Volunteers launched the nonprofit Twinberry Commons to help the transition [1]. Burgwin retired in October 2025 and sold his assets to the new co-op [1]. Community members chose the name Blackberry Food Cooperative; it was formally incorporated in May 2025, had 246 member-owners by the end of 2025, and began operating under co-op ownership in April 2026 [1][3]. The Register-Guard reported the community purchased the farm stand on April 1, and that the store is open to everyone, not just members [2].

Membership is a one-time $200, payable in installments of as little as $20 a month, and members can vote for the board and on what goes on the shelves [1]. By late October 2026, membership stood at 366 and was growing by the week [1]. Blackberry sits inside Cottage Grove Public Market, alongside local food entrepreneurs, workshops on foraging and food preservation, a small pub and a food truck pod [1]. Its next goal is buying the building, with a capital campaign in preparation, and a fundraiser is set for November 28, 2 to 6 p.m., with the Willamette Farm & Food Coalition [1].

No bailout. No chain swooping in. A town that needed a grocery did the arithmetic itself — $200 a family, $20 a month if that's what you've got — and now owns the store, the shelves and the decisions. That is common-man culture in its working clothes: not a program somebody in Salem or Washington designed for these people, but the people themselves, with a caravan of cars full of honey and bulk grains.
