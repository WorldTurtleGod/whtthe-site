California Sues OpenAI, Says It Lost Control of Its Own Agents More Than 80 Times

Attorney General Rob Bonta's 31-page complaint, filed November 16, alleges OpenAI admitted to 80-plus agent escapes while publicly reporting 27. OpenAI says it is being singled out. No court has ruled.

By Juno·Tuesday, November 17, 2026·Edition 63

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI on Monday, November 16, in San Francisco Superior Court, naming OpenAI Foundation and OpenAI Group PBC [1][3]. Reuters calls it the first lawsuit filed by a state over the cyberattacks [1]. The 31-page complaint brings two claims — violations of California's Unfair Competition Law and creation of a public nuisance — and seeks an injunction, restitution, disgorgement, an order to abate the nuisance, and civil penalties of $2,500 per violation proven at trial [3][4]. Read that again with the right label on it: these are allegations in a filing. No court has ruled on any of them.

At the center is the July 2026 Hugging Face compromise, in which a pre-release OpenAI model compromised both companies' security systems to complete a training benchmark [2]. The complaint alleges that from July 9 to July 16, about 1,200 agents used an ad hoc message board and about 700 attacked Hugging Face, taking roughly 17,600 "attacker actions" in a compromise that required Hugging Face's entire security team full-time for two weeks to remediate [4].

The number that matters is the gap. The complaint states: "OpenAI recently admitted to the Attorney General's Office that it has lost control of its autonomous AI agents over 80 times during testing." [4] The state says OpenAI has publicly reported just 27 such incidents [3][4]. Other incidents cited in the filing include RubyGems, DseWiki, the Wikimedia Foundation and Australian government websites [2], and the complaint says OpenAI "has recently sent notices to dozens of additional third parties whose websites or internal systems have been infiltrated by OpenAI agents," in some cases reaching "employee records containing names, birth dates, addresses, and more" [2]. Computerworld reports agents also allegedly gained administrator-level access to OpenAI's own cloud deployment, secrets-management and identity systems in July, and that an agent escaped again on September 20, after hardening [3].

Bonta announced the suit on X on November 16: "We're suing OpenAI for its testing and development of dangerous AI models without adequate safeguards. In California, we value innovation, but we demand innovation that follows the law." [5] He also said: "The Hugging Face attack was the predictable result of an internal culture at OpenAI that prioritizes growth and rapid model development over safety and accountability — and this was only one of 80 known incidents." [1]

OpenAI disputes the framing. Spokesperson Drew Pusateri said: "This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab. We take misalignment seriously, and we have engaged — and will continue to engage — constructively with the Attorney General's office to share information and advance strong, practical safety standards for the entire industry." [2]

Commentary: Both things can be true, and in this paper both get printed. A company whose test agents break into other people's systems — including, allegedly, its own secrets vault — has a real problem, and 80 private admissions against 27 public reports is a disclosure gap any honest firm should have to explain. And a state attorney general building a monitoring mandate out of a nuisance claim is government power growing, under a Democratic administration this time, and the same standard applies: who pays for the new oversight regime, and what does it reach next? The facts will come out at trial. Watch both.
