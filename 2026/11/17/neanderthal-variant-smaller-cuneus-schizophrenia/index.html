A Piece of Neanderthal DNA Is Still Shaping Human Brains. It Also Cuts Schizophrenia Risk.

Svante Pääbo and Hugo Zeberg traced a four-letter deletion on chromosome 14 back to Neanderthals. Carriers have a smaller visual region at the back of the brain, and two copies go with a nearly 10% lower schizophrenia risk.

By Publius·Tuesday, November 17, 2026·Edition 63

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Neanderthals died out about 40,000 years ago. A small piece of their DNA is still changing the shape of living people's brains.

In a study published Monday, Nov. 16, in Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, evolutionary geneticists Hugo Zeberg and Svante Pääbo report that a common genetic variant affecting the volume of the occipital lobe, the part of the brain at the back of the head that handles vision, was inherited from Neanderthals. [1][2] Pääbo won the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for his work sequencing ancient human genomes. [3]

Nature reports that the variant is tiny: a deletion of four DNA letters on chromosome 14, first spotted in 2019. [1] According to ScienceAlert, earlier genome studies had found four spots in the human genome linked to occipital volume. Three turned up in sub-Saharan African populations. This one was found only outside that region, where Neanderthal DNA is common because Neanderthals interbred with the ancestors of those populations. [2]

That was a clue, not proof. So the researchers checked ancient DNA. All five detailed Neanderthal genomes carried the deletion. The Denisovans, another extinct human group, did not. [1][2] To rule out a much older common ancestor, they measured the stretch of DNA around it. It runs 59.7 kilobases, nearly 60,000 letters, which is too long to have survived hundreds of thousands of years of genetic reshuffling. It came in recently, through interbreeding. "This is a piece of DNA that looks like it comes from Neanderthals," Zeberg told Nature. [1][2]

The effect runs the opposite way from what you might guess. Neanderthals are thought to have had larger occipital lobes than we do. But in people today, each copy of the Neanderthal variant goes with 0.85 cubic centimeters less volume in a part of the occipital lobe called the cuneus. Nature reports that is a bigger effect on the cuneus than any other known variant. [1] Scientific American puts the total for people with two copies, one from each parent, at 1.7 cubic centimeters. [3] Zeberg's explanation, to ScienceAlert: "Brain anatomy is influenced by many genes and other factors, including eye and body size. A single Neanderthal variant need not reflect the overall anatomy of Neanderthals." [2]

Then the surprise. People with two copies had a nearly 10% lower risk of schizophrenia, according to Scientific American. [3] The researchers found no link to depression, bipolar disorder or ADHD, and no link to intelligence or general cognitive ability. [2][3] The deletion sits near a gene called DAAM1, which plays a role in early brain development, but how it acts on that gene is not yet known. [2][3]

Outside scientists were impressed. "Its effect is really one of the most beautiful and strong that I've ever seen," computational biologist Davide Marnetto of the University of Turin told Nature. [1] Nadav Ahituv, director of the Institute for Human Genetics at UC San Francisco, who reviewed the study, called it "fascinating." [3]

The pair say they are not done combing the genome for other Neanderthal pieces that matter. "You might hear from us again," Zeberg told Scientific American. [3]
