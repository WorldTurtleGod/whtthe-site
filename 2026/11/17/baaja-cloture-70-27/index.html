70 to 27: The Senate Just Voted to Stop Debating Whether America Can Build

Cloture on the Capito permitting substitute passed Monday night — the second of three 60-vote gates. The survivor-relief bill underneath it was not gutted; it rides on as Division C.

By Juno·Tuesday, November 17, 2026·Edition 63

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

The Senate voted 70–27 on Monday evening, November 16, to invoke cloture on the Capito substitute amendment #6883 to H.R. 2347, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act [1][2]. Sixty votes were needed. The vote came at about 6:27 p.m. ET, roll call 264, with three senators not voting [2][3].

Keep the votes straight, because Washington won't. This was not final passage, and it was not the November 9 vote. On November 9 the Senate voted 73–25 to end debate on the motion to proceed — to start talking. Monday's vote ends debate on the substitute itself, the actual 494-page text released November 7 [2][3]. One gate remains: cloture on the underlying bill, expected early Wednesday, then a simple-majority vote on final passage, expected before Thanksgiving [2][3]. Then the House, which must pass the same text before the 119th Congress expires on January 3, 2027, or the whole thing starts over [2].

What is in the text: deadlines for reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act, the Clean Water Act and the Endangered Species Act, limits on the lawsuits used to stall projects, and a rule that data centers pay for the power and grid upgrades they cause instead of spreading those costs onto households [2][3]. The bill's authors are Senators Shelley Moore Capito (R-W.Va.), Mike Lee (R-Utah), Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.) and Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.) [2]. The party split on Monday, per a publicly posted roll-call tally, tells its own story: Republicans voted 49–2 for cloture, Democrats 20–24 against, with the two independents split [4].

Now the vehicle, because you will hear it lied about this week. H.R. 2347 is the Survivor Justice Tax Prevention Act — its text excludes from gross income damages, other than punitive damages, received on account of sexual acts or sexual contact [5]. Activist posts claimed the substitute emptied that bill out and replaced it. The substitute's own text review says otherwise: the survivor provision is kept as Division C, with permitting placed in front of it [6]. A survivor-relief bill is being used as the legislative vehicle — that maneuver is real, and worth naming — but the relief was not deleted. Anyone who tells you it was gutted is selling you something.

The opposition is real too. The substitute drops two provisions tribes opposed most, but tribal objections to the Section 106 historic-preservation rewrite remain [2]. Nearly half the Senate Democratic caucus opposes the bill, and incoming House Speaker Hakeem Jeffries has said the Senate product falls short [3].

Commentary: This paper's standard does not change with the party in charge. A permitting regime that lets any project be sued into a decade of delay is government power too — power handed to whoever can afford the lawyers. Cutting it back is the rare Washington move that shrinks a veto instead of growing a program. But watch the two questions we ask of every bill: who pays, and what freedom does it cost? The data-center cost rule is the answer to the first — the companies causing the grid costs pay them, not your electric bill. If that rule gets traded away in the House, you will read it here first.
