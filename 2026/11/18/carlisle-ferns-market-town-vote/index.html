A Town With No Grocery Store Voted, Late Into the Night, to Bring Its Store Back

Carlisle, Massachusetts, has no grocery store and no coffee shop since Ferns Market closed in May 2025. On November 16 a record town-meeting turnout approved the engineering money to fix the wastewater problem that killed it — while a couple who still don't own the place have $100,000 of their own money in.

Ferns Market is all Carlisle has, and right now Carlisle doesn't have it. The general store at 8 Lowell Street — a store has stood on that corner since 1844, the current building dating to 1925 — closed in May 2025, leaving the Massachusetts town with no grocery store and no coffee shop [1][2]. On Monday, November 16, a record turnout at a Special Town Meeting overwhelmingly approved Article 6, funding the next phase of engineering, design and permitting — an estimated $225,000 to $300,000 — for a wastewater connection between Carlisle center and the town's existing treatment facility at Banta Davis [1][2]. The Pirris say more than 85 percent of voters supported it, in a meeting that ran nearly five hours [2].

Amy Pirri was standing in that room. "I was holding back tears. It was just amazing to feel that and know that so many people were there," she said [1]. Her case for the store is arithmetic, not nostalgia: "Ferns is all we have. There's no grocery store. There's no coffee shops. There's nowhere else to buy a bottle of water or a cup of coffee or anything in the whole town." [1] And: "And it just sounds like a store, but it's so much more than that because it's all the community has." [1]

The couple behind this are not owners — not yet. Amy and Ben Pirri stopped at Ferns after signing the papers on their Carlisle home; when it closed, someone asked Ben if he wanted to buy it. "I said, 'Yeah. I'm absolutely interested,'" he recalled [1]. Eighteen months later they hold a tentative purchase agreement and have put more than $100,000 of their own money into investigating whether it can reopen at all [1][2]. What they found: title issues, 17 lienholders, environmental oversight from the property's gas-station era and a petroleum cleanup in 2000 — and above all, wastewater [1]. Ferns ran on a variance with a leaching system sized at a fraction of its approved flow, a composting toilet, seating limits and food prep forced off site, and that variance does not transfer to a new owner [1][2]. A traditional septic does not fit the small center lots amid wells and drinking-water protections; an engineering study found the Banta Davis school facility appears to have capacity for Ferns and the Gleason Public Library while preserving the school's needs, with state approval still required [1].

Ben Pirri's answer to the obvious objection is the honest one: "It's a wastewater solution for Town Center which enables the center to have a business like that." [1] The objection itself belongs in print: the Carlisle Board of Health opposed Article 6, saying it lacked the research, planning and stakeholder input to justify town funding, and called for a broader water and wastewater assessment [1]. Some residents initially saw it as taxpayers financing a sewer hookup for one private business [1].

Nothing has reopened, and this paper won't pretend otherwise. Spring 2027 voters still decide whether to fund construction, estimated at $460,000 to $1.1 million, with state grants to be pursued off the Article 6 engineering [1][2]. The Pirris pay their share of Ferns' own connection, won't complete the purchase until construction is authorized, and are taking the next risk themselves in the meantime — architectural drawings, permitting, renovation planning [1][2]. If the spring vote passes, What Now Boston reports, the state has indicated it would consider letting Ferns reopen on a temporary holding tank while the permanent connection is built, putting a possible reopening in fall 2027 [2]. Their survey of nearly 600 residents says what the town wants back: 86 percent a casual meeting place, 85 percent counter service, 76 percent café seating — a general store and fast-casual kitchen, breakfast, lunch and dinner seven days a week, 30 to 40 seats, exterior essentially unchanged [1][2]. The couple acknowledge it may never be a big moneymaker [1]. "We need to bring this back to the community," Ben said. "We need to bring this back for our own family." [1]

Commentary: Apply this paper's two questions honestly, because the town money is real: who pays? Carlisle's taxpayers voted — in person, in record numbers, staying nearly five hours — to fund engineering for shared town-center infrastructure, while the couple pays its own connection, its own drawings and its own $100,000 of risk, and the construction bill still faces its own vote in the spring. That is what consent to spend looks like at the scale where you can see it. No agency absorbed this store. A town argued about it in the open, voted, and a family put its own money where its mouth is.
