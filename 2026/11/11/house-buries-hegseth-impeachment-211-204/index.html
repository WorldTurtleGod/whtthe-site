House Buries Hegseth Impeachment 211–204 — Without Voting on the War Charges

Rep. Thomas Massie forced the House to confront eight articles accusing Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth of waging the Iran war without Congress. Republicans sent the resolution to committee instead — a procedural kill, not an acquittal.

By Juno·Wednesday, November 11, 2026·Edition 57

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

On Monday, November 9, Rep. Thomas Massie, R-Kentucky, filed eight articles of impeachment against Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, as H.Res. 1655 — his second attempt, after the House went into recess before a vote on his September filing. On Tuesday, November 10, the House voted 211–204, Roll Call 317, at 6:02 p.m. ET, on a motion by Rep. Darrell Issa, R-California, to send the resolution to the House Judiciary Committee. That effectively halted it for this Congress. [1][2]

Read that vote for what it was. It was not a vote on whether Hegseth committed the alleged offences, and it was not an acquittal. It was a procedural referral. 210 Republicans and one independent voted yes to referral; 203 Democrats and Massie — the only Republican voting no — voted against it. 18 members did not vote: 7 Republicans and 11 Democrats. [2][3]

The first three articles go to the oldest question in the book: who decides war. They allege Hegseth violated the Constitution by directing U.S. forces to sustain hostilities against Iran without congressional approval, citing the War Powers Act, which requires the president to end combat operations after 60 days unless Congress authorizes them. USA TODAY notes the House and Senate have both adopted resolutions instructing Trump to end the Iran war, but none has been binding. [1]

The fourth article alleges Hegseth ignored laws requiring the military to minimize civilian casualties. Massie cited the Feb. 28 U.S. missile strike on the Shajareh Tayyebeh primary school in Iran, on the first day of the Iran war, which he said killed at least 168 people, the majority children. The Pentagon has yet to release findings of its investigation into that strike. [1]

The remaining articles allege unlawful "extrajudicial killings" of alleged drug traffickers in the Caribbean, risking U.S. lives in the operation to capture Venezuelan leader Nicolás Maduro, and military action in Yemen without congressional approval. Massie described the Maduro operation as an "act of war against a sovereign country executed under the guise of narcotics law enforcement." U.S. Southern Command has targeted suspected drug boats since September 2025; USA TODAY reports the U.S. military has killed more than 240 people in that campaign, while the Pentagon has provided scant evidence the boats carried narcotics. [1]

The final article accuses Hegseth of violating the First Amendment and weaponizing the Pentagon against Sen. Mark Kelly, D-Arizona. In November 2025 Kelly appeared in a video telling troops they can refuse illegal orders; Trump accused the lawmakers of "seditious behavior, punishable by death." Hegseth censured Kelly and ordered a Pentagon investigation that could reduce his military rank. Kelly sued in January; a federal judge sided with Kelly, and the Justice Department has appealed. [1][4]

Massie, to reporters on Nov. 9: "We're not indicting him for being bad at his job. The argument here is that he's broken laws and he's violated the Constitution." Hegseth, at a Pentagon briefing on Nov. 10, called it "a stunt and everybody knows it," and said: "The claim that the American military targets civilians, well, that is a lie and that is a slander against every professional who flew, sailed, and fought on these missions." Speaker Mike Johnson said before the vote: "We have the votes. We'll dispense with the impeachment gambit." [1][2]

What happens next is a calendar, not a verdict. Any article passing the House on a simple majority would go to the Senate, where dismissal would take a simple majority but conviction and removal would take two-thirds. Only two Cabinet members have ever been impeached by the House, and neither was convicted in the Senate. Democrats take control of both chambers in January 2027, when the effort could be revived; Massie, per CBS as reported in secondary coverage, would not file another impeachment resolution but planned to force a vote on a war powers resolution. [1][3]

That is the constitutional shape of this story, and it is the one the founders wrote down: Congress declares war, the president commands it, and when the two disagree, the fight is supposed to happen in the open, on the record. On Tuesday it happened on a procedural motion instead — 211 to 204, and the charges themselves were never debated.
