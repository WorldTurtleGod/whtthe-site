Webb's "Little Red Dots" May Be the Birthplaces of the First Supermassive Black Holes

New simulations say the hundreds of tiny, puzzling red specks Webb found in the early universe are primeval galaxies hiding newborn black holes inside dense gas — a model that finally explains their strange light.

By Juno·Wednesday, November 11, 2026·Edition 57

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Since the James Webb Space Telescope began looking deep into the early universe, astronomers have found hundreds of compact, bright objects known as "little red dots" — over 300 have now been discovered, per the paper behind this story. They are only a few hundred light-years across, yet are as bright as much larger galaxies; they have unusual V-shaped spectra and no detectable X-ray or radio emissions. For years, that combination defied the usual explanations. [1][2][3]

On November 10, the University of Portsmouth and Phys.org reported a new explanation. Using advanced cosmological simulations, a University of Portsmouth-led team found little red dots are most likely primeval galaxies hosting massive black holes formed by the collapse of supermassive primordial stars — so-called direct-collapse black holes. [1][2]

The hiding mechanism is the elegant part. The black holes remain wrapped inside the enormous gas disks that created them. Dense gas traps high-energy X-rays while allowing some ultraviolet, visible and infrared light to escape, recreating the light signatures Webb sees. The simulated black holes have masses around 10 million times that of the Sun. The host galaxies also contain dense clusters of around 100 million stars packed into a region only around 500 light-years across, matching real little-red-dot observations. [1][3]

The paper, published in The Astrophysical Journal Letters on October 29, 2026, says little red dots sit at redshifts 4 to 8, and the model reproduces spectra from typical examples such as RUBIES-EGS-42046 at redshift 5.28 to the most distant, CAPERS-LRD-z9 at redshift 9.29. [3]

One caveat, kept where it belongs: this is a simulation result whose modelled spectra match observations. Nobody has directly watched a black hole being born. Previous theories — compact star collections, heavily obscured black holes, supermassive stars, or hypothetical "black hole stars" — could not fully explain the dots' formation, abundance, lifespan and spectra. [1][2]

Lead author Dr. Daniel Whalen put it plainly: "What makes this discovery so exciting is that we may be witnessing the birth of the first supermassive black holes. For decades, simulations have predicted that these objects should exist, and now the James Webb Space Telescope may finally be allowing us to see them." [1][2]

LRDs have been one of the most surprising discoveries made by the James Webb Space Telescope. They are compact, only a few hundred light-years across, but are as bright as much larger galaxies, and their unusual V-shaped spectra have defied the usual astrophysical explanations. Their lack of detectable X-ray and radio emissions has left astronomers struggling to explain what they are.
