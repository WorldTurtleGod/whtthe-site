The Same Building Block of Life, Born in Two Different Neighborhoods of the Early Solar System

Beta-alanine from asteroid Bennu and from a meteorite that fell in 1969 seems to have formed the same way, but its atoms carry fingerprints from different places. One Bennu carbon holds the biggest isotope oddity ever recorded in an organic molecule from space.

By Herodotus·Wednesday, November 11, 2026·Edition 57

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Two travelers can speak the same language and still give away, by accent, that they grew up in different towns. Penn State researchers say they have found the chemical version of that accent inside an amino acid from space.

The team studied beta-alanine in two samples. One came from asteroid Bennu, delivered to Earth in 2023 by NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission and free of earthly contamination. The other came from the Murchison meteorite, which fell to Earth in 1969. Beta-alanine isn't one of the 20 amino acids that build proteins, but it is one of the most common amino acids found in asteroid and meteorite samples. [1]

Most isotope studies measure a molecule's average. This team developed a technique to measure the carbon isotopes at each position inside the molecule, using a Penn State instrument called the pico-CSIA, which the university says exists nowhere else, together with a modified GC-Orbitrap. [1]

The averages were almost identical. "When you compare the Bennu and Murchison samples, the carbon isotope average is so close that when you factor in margin of error, they're basically the same," said lead author Ophélie Mcintosh, a former Penn State postdoctoral researcher. "But when you look at intramolecular carbon, they're completely different." [1]

In both samples, one of beta-alanine's three carbons is heavily enriched in heavier isotopes while the other two are depleted. The pattern matches between the samples, but the values do not. The enriched carbon in the Bennu sample is the largest isotope anomaly ever recorded in an organic molecule from space, Penn State says. [1] The paper reports position-by-position differences of roughly 100 to 250 parts per thousand between the two samples. The authors say those gaps are hard to explain with known processes on the parent asteroids and may be direct evidence that organic chemistry varied from place to place across the disk that formed the planets. [2]

So, same recipe, different kitchens. A similar structure suggests a shared way of forming. Different values suggest different places. "I was genuinely gobsmacked by the enormous isotope difference between two neighboring atom positions within the same molecule," said Katherine Freeman, the project's principal investigator. "We are working with molecules formed at the very dawn of the solar system, possibly even before the formation of Earth." [1]

It fits the team's earlier work on glycine from Bennu, which suggested that amino acid may have formed in a harsh, cold environment. That runs against the long-held idea that life's building blocks formed in warm, wet conditions. [1]

The paper itself appeared in Communications Earth & Environment on September 24. Penn State publicized it this week. [1][2] OSIRIS-REx has finished its mission, but the questions it brought home are far from settled. Next, Freeman says, the team wants to learn how processes in space, such as exposure to water, rearrange a molecule's isotopes. [1]
