The White House Tells Congress Its Questions Might Be Crimes

In a letter to the incoming House Oversight chair, the White House counsel warned that Democratic investigations "may cross the line into blatant criminality." The first House to investigate a president's men did it in 1792, and the republic survived the questions.

By Herodotus·Wednesday, November 11, 2026·Edition 57

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

There is an old order to things in Washington. An election changes who holds the gavels, the new majority announces what it means to look into, and the administration replies that it will not enjoy being looked into. This week the reply came early, and in an unusual form.

In a letter dated Monday, November 9, White House counsel Will Scharf wrote to Rep. Robert Garcia of California, the top Democrat on the House Oversight Committee and its expected chair in the next Congress. Punchbowl News first reported the letter on Wednesday, and HuffPost obtained a copy. [1][2]

The letter makes the familiar argument first: Congress may only investigate for a legislative purpose, and Scharf says the coming Democratic probes are designed to "harass and aggrandize rather than to advance any legitimate legislative end." Then it goes further than custom. "Threats to executive branch officials designed to deter them from public service, or process designed merely to abuse or instill fear in the recipient may cross the line into blatant criminality," Scharf wrote. "And issuing unlawful subpoenas with the intention of depriving citizens of their constitutional rights and privileges may warrant further investigation." [1][2]

Scharf points to Garcia's own words, including his public advice that administration officials should "lawyer up," which the letter treats as malicious and improper. The letter did not arrive alone. On Saturday, President Trump wrote on Truth Social that Democrats' threats to investigate were "illegal" and that he was "ordering the Department of Justice to look into this criminal act, immediately!" Attorney General Todd Blanche echoed the claim in a weekend CNN interview. [1][2]

Garcia was not moved. "It appears Donald Trump has something to hide," he said. "Let's be clear: Congress' authority comes from Article I of the Constitution, not from the permission of the White House. Congress has wide latitude to conduct investigations and oversight, an authority inherent in the power to legislate." [2]

Two facts frame the threat. HuffPost notes that the Supreme Court has repeatedly held that the power to investigate is part of the legislative process itself. And this administration has already tried to prosecute Democratic lawmakers: grand juries refused to indict six members who appeared in a video urging troops to disobey illegal orders, while other cases remain open. Punchbowl's own read is blunt: the letter is unlikely to deter Democrats, but it sets the tone for 2027. [1][2]

Commentary. A chronicler likes to know how a quarrel began, and this one began early. On November 4, 1791, an American Indian confederacy routed U.S. troops under Maj. Gen. Arthur St. Clair in the Northwest Territory. The next spring the House opened its first investigation of the executive branch. When it asked the War and State Departments for papers, President George Washington, of all people, at first held some back. Then he relented, and the House committee laid the blame on the War Department for failing to equip and train the army. [3]

Notice what did not happen. Washington did not tell the House that asking was a crime. He held some papers back, then gave ground and let the inquiry run. The founders built the government on the expectation of exactly that friction. "Ambition must be made to counteract ambition," Publius wrote in Federalist No. 51, in February 1788, because "if men were angels, no government would be necessary." [4] Oversight is not a favor the executive grants to Congress. It is one of the counterweights the Constitution hangs on the executive, and it works whichever party holds the gavel.

Congress can certainly abuse that power. Fishing expeditions are real, and a subpoena can be a weapon. The remedy the system offers is a fight over scope, privilege, and the courts, conducted in the open. Treating the questions themselves as a possible crime is a different thing. It asks the branch being watched to decide whether the watchers may be prosecuted. The founders would have recognized that arrangement, and they would not have called it a republic.
