Typhoid's Deadliest Wound Was a Blood Vessel Problem All Along

For more than a century, doctors could describe how typhoid fever tears the small intestine but not why. A Yale lab that has chased one bacterial toxin for 20 years says it finally has the answer.

By Herodotus·Saturday, November 14, 2026·Edition 60

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Typhoid fever is one of those diseases that medicine has known by sight for a very long time. What it never knew was why the worst of it happens.

The most lethal complication is a hole in the small intestine. It shows up late in the illness, and it kills even after antibiotics have been given. Researchers at Yale School of Medicine now report, in a study published in Nature Microbiology, that the cause is a toxin. Typhoid toxin, made by the bacterium Salmonella Typhi, circulates in the bloodstream and damages the small blood vessels of the small intestine, leaving them too weak to hold up when the bacteria arrive later. [1]

"Intestinal perforation, it turns out, is a disease of blood vessels," the Yale announcement says. [1]

"This is a problem solved," said Jorge E. Galán, whose laboratory discovered typhoid toxin about two decades ago and has been assembling the pieces since. [1]

The finding ties together symptoms that looked unrelated. Typhoid can cause neurological illness while there are almost no bacteria in the blood, and its intestinal perforations stay confined to the lower small intestine. The team says these share one origin: the toxin reaching the lining of different networks of tiny blood vessels. The disease's typical drop in white blood cells is also driven by the toxin, they say. "The same activity, in a different vascular bed, produces a completely different clinical picture," Galán said. [1]

The work was done in mice, and that is part of the news. Salmonella Typhi infects only humans, so scientists have never had an animal model that reproduces the intestinal disease seen in patients. Now they do, which means antitoxin antibodies, vaccines and drugs that protect blood vessels can be tested against this complication. For the first time, the researchers say, treatments that neutralize the toxin and shield blood vessels, given alongside antibiotics, are "a realistic prospect." [1]

The stakes are not small. Typhoid still kills an estimated 200,000 people a year, mostly where vaccines and good treatment are scarce, and most of the dead are children and the elderly. [1] Heng Zhao, a postdoctoral researcher in the Galán lab, was first author, and the National Institutes of Health funded the work. [1]

Commentary. There is something satisfying about a lab working one question for twenty years and getting to say the word "solved." Patience like that rarely makes headlines until the day it does. The usual caution applies: this is mouse work, and the treatments it points to still have to be built and tested in people.
