America Sat Still for Four Hours — and the Bill Arrived the Same Day

A telecommunications failure grounded every departing flight in the country on Friday. The White House had asked Congress for $30.1 billion that morning. By nightfall, the ask was $50 billion.

By Juno·Saturday, November 14, 2026·Edition 60

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

At 3:36 p.m. Eastern on Friday, Nov. 13, the radar and communications data running America's air traffic control system started contradicting itself. Switches and routers in the FAA's aging analog network overloaded, the data went inconsistent across the agency's 22 en route centers, and the FAA did the only safe thing left: it stopped everything. [1][2]

The nationwide ground stop — only the third in American history, after 9/11 and a January 2023 outage — lasted more than four hours. More than 3,500 airplanes were in the air when it was issued; by the time it lifted at 8:15 p.m. Eastern, fewer than 400 were. Thousands of flights were canceled, crews timed out, and delays were still rippling through Saturday morning, Nov. 14. [1][2][3]

Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy did not dress it up. "It was a legacy network problem," he said Friday night, blaming old hardware and 1980s-era software. The FAA "deployed fixes" and replaced failed switches, but Duffy warned the country plainly: "You will have more of these." [2][4]

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said the public was never in danger and there was no evidence of a cyberattack. "This is a national security risk," he said, adding that the president had declared the state of the system a national emergency. [2]

Here is the part Washington will fight over. The failure was not a surprise the administration discovered Friday afternoon. That morning — hours before the ground stop — Duffy and White House budget director Russell Vought had already written to Congress asking for an additional $30.1 billion to modernize the system: $10.1 billion for air traffic control, $2 billion for towers, $18 billion for airports, on top of the $12.5 billion Congress approved in 2025. By Friday night, Duffy had raised his number in public: "The president's asked for $30 billion," he said. "I want $50 billion." And again: "This is an emergency. I want Congress to give us emergency funding." [1][4][5]

The timing is the politics. Republicans lost the midterms on Nov. 3, and Democrats take control of Congress in January. The administration is asking a lame-duck Congress it still holds to fund the rebuild before the gavel changes hands — and Friday's empty skies are its best argument. [5] Democrats, for their part, are not simply saying no: Sen. Tammy Duckworth, the ranking Democrat on the Senate aviation subcommittee, called for a thorough investigation into how one telecommunications failure could ground a nation, and Rep. Veronica Escobar took to X to ask why a party that controlled Washington let the system rot to this point. [3][6]

Both questions deserve answers, and they are not the same question. A control system running on overloaded analog switches is a failure decades in the making, built by both parties kicking the can. But $50 billion is real money, and "emergency" is the word Washington uses when it does not want to count. Congress should fund the modernization — and it should demand, in writing, where the $12.5 billion already approved has gone, what the new money buys line by line, and who answers when the next router overloads. The planes stopped for four hours. The asking should not stop being questioned just because the stopping was scary.
