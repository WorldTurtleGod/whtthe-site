Claude and a Mathematician Claim the Riemann Hypothesis. The Code Checks. The Prize Hasn't Moved.

On Friday, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge announced he and Claude had proved the Riemann hypothesis, posed in 1859 — releasing the argument as Lean code, not a paper. On Saturday, independent researchers said they had recompiled and certified it. The Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the problem as unsolved, and this paper prints all three facts in the same breath.

On Friday, November 20, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, claimed to have proven the Riemann hypothesis using the company's Claude model [1]. His statement, posted on his website: "I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis" [1]. The hypothesis, first proposed in 1859 by Bernhard Riemann, concerns the distribution of prime numbers, and carries a $1-million Clay Mathematics Institute bounty [1]. Alpöge posted the proof coded in Lean, the programming language that verifies the mathematical correctness of each argument, and says it confirms the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis as well [1]. CNN reports the release runs to some 17 million lines of Lean code, with a full prose write-up that would exceed 250,000 pages not released [2]. USA Today reports he worked with Claude for approximately six weeks [3]. The announcement came, Scientific American notes, "just a day after Anthropic's record-breaking debut on the stock market" [1] — timing this paper reports without endorsing any motive for it. Anthropic did not respond to comment requests from multiple outlets [2][3].

On Saturday, November 21, the claim moved from announcement to machine check. The Lean and Mathlib communities issued a joint statement: "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, and we congratulate him on this achievement." "We hereby certify that both the statement and the proof of the Riemann Hypothesis are correct" [4]. Andrew Sutherland, a mathematician at MIT, separately compiled the result without issues: "The Lean proof has been compiled and independently rechecked by the Lean kernel with only the three standard axioms." "Honestly the chance that anything is fishy here is approximately zero" [4]. A comparator run — checking that the code does not redefine symbols or exploit kernel bugs — has been completed [4].

Now the caveats, in ink, because they decide what this story is. Machine-checked is not human-understood, and neither is Clay-accepted. The Clay Mathematics Institute still lists the Riemann hypothesis as unsolved, and under Clay rules a solution must be published in a peer-reviewed journal and withstand two years of scrutiny [2][3]. Alpöge himself, per CNN, says he has not mastered the mathematics and is presenting a complete argument before having mastered a massive neighbourhood around it [2]. Hours after his announcement, a rival 940-page human-written draft proof by Alessandro Masullo surfaced, developed without AI; whether it holds remains to be seen [5]. So: no outlet in this reporting claims mathematicians have digested the ideas. What is documented is narrower and still enormous — a Claude-assisted proof, released as code, that independent kernels recompile and independent mathematicians certify states the real theorem.

Commentary: Two questions. Who pays? Nobody, yet — no prize has moved, no journal has accepted, and the two-year clock has not started. What does it cost? Watch the understanding gap. A proof no human has fully read, verified by machines checking machines, is a new kind of knowledge: correct, certified, and not yet comprehended. Sutherland, who ran the check himself, called it "a human triumph, and a day for celebration" while noting "there is much work to be done to understand the mathematical details of the proof" [1]. Both halves belong in print. The machine did something the smartest people alive agreed they were nowhere close to. Whether mathematics can absorb what it did is the story of the next two years — and this paper will print the Clay verdict either way.
