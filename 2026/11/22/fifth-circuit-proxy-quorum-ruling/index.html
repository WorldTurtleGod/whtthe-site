The House Passed a $1.7 Trillion Law Without a Quorum, the Fifth Circuit Says

On Friday, the en banc Fifth Circuit ruled 9–8 that the House violated the Constitution's Quorum Clause when it passed the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 by counting 226 proxy-recorded members toward a quorum. The judgment itself is narrow — one pregnancy-workers provision, unenforceable against Texas — but the reasoning points at everything else in that omnibus.

By Juno·Sunday, November 22, 2026·Edition 68

On Friday, November 20, the en banc U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit ruled 9–8 in State of Texas v. Blanche that the House of Representatives violated the Constitution's Quorum Clause when it passed the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023 [1][2]. Judge Cory Wilson wrote for the majority, joined by Chief Judge Elrod and Judges Jones, Smith, Willett, Ho, Duncan, Engelhardt, and Oldham [2]. Reuters, reporting the ruling the same day, put the vote and the holding plainly: the court "voted 9-8 to uphold an injunction" secured by Texas, holding "that the proxy rule violated the US Constitution's quorum clause, which the court said requires a majority of House members to be physically present to pass legislation" [1].

The vote at issue: the House passed the $1.7 trillion spending package on December 23, 2022, by 225–201, with one member recorded present and four non-votes — and 226 members voting by proxy [1][2]. The majority's count, from the opinion: "Of the 427 Members who recorded a vote on the Act, the Congressional Record indicates that 226 Members—a majority of the House—had recorded their presence and their vote by proxy," and so "when the House voted on the Act, less than a majority of Members were physically present" [2]. The rule that allowed it — H.Res. 965, adopted May 15, 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, and renewed in H.Res. 8 in the 117th Congress — let an absent member designate a proxy to cast a vote or record presence, with proxy-recorded presence counted toward a quorum [2]. It was, Reuters notes, "ditched when Republicans took control of the House after the November 2022 elections" — but not before this vote [1].

The majority's reasoning, in its own words: "We conclude that the enrolled-bill rule does not bar our review because Texas's constitutional challenge does not implicate a dispute of fact regarding the Act's passage. We further conclude that the Act's passage was unconstitutional because the Quorum Clause does not allow proxies to be included when establishing a quorum" [2]. The clause's "text, context, original understanding, and unbroken historical application all indicate that Members of Congress must be present for quorum purposes" [2]. And the sentence that will be quoted for years: "While the chambers of the legislature have much discretion regarding their rules of procedure, the Quorum Clause's constraints on congressional power cannot be circumvented by congressional fiat" [1][2]. "Proxy presence" does not count [2].

Now the scope, printed exactly as narrow as the court printed it. Texas, under outgoing Attorney General Ken Paxton, challenged just one portion of the Act — the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act — and the judgment affirms an injunction barring the Department of Justice and the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission from enforcing that provision against Texas [1][2]. The court emphasized "the narrow scope of our judgment" and stated its decision "extends no further than affirming the relief awarded by the district court" [2]. The PWFA remains in force elsewhere. The same omnibus also carried the Electoral Count Reform and Presidential Transition Improvement Act, the No TikTok on Government Devices Act, and the SECURE 2.0 Act — whether this reasoning reaches those provisions is contested, not decided, and this paper does not print them as invalidated [2].

The dissent gets its lines too, because they are part of the record. Judge James Graves, joined by Judges Stewart, Richman, Southwick, Higginson, Douglas, and Ramirez, called the ruling "illogical, inexplicable, and invalid," arguing the majority read a physical-presence requirement into the Quorum Clause that does not exist — and asked why, if the whole law was unconstitutionally passed, the judgment "should be limited only to one small sliver of the multitrillion dollar bill" [1][2]. Judge Haynes dissented separately, stating she does not think it appropriate "to throw out the Federal House of Representatives in this arena" [2]. The Justice Department, which defended the law, and Paxton's office did not respond to requests for comment [1]. No Supreme Court petition had been announced in the reporting checked for this edition; commentators call review likely, which is a prediction, not a filing.

Grade this exactly as it sits: the judgment is documented — a filed en banc opinion, corroborated the same day by Reuters [1][2]. What it means for the rest of the omnibus, for videoconference presence (a question the court expressly left open), and for the Supreme Court is reported and contested, not settled.

Commentary: Apply this paper's standard, same for both parties, in both directions. A House that can declare absent members present by rule has written itself an exception to a constitutional constraint — power growing by fiat, exactly what Judge Wilson's sentence names. And nine judges telling the people's House what "present" means is judicial power growing over internal House procedure, which is why Judge Haynes's one-line dissent deserves its place in print. Who pays? Today, Texas employers and pregnant workers under one provision in one state. Tomorrow, if the reasoning holds, every future House majority trying to legislate in an emergency — and every litigant holding a law passed on December 23, 2022. What freedom does it cost, either way? The Quorum Clause exists so a rump cannot legislate for the whole. The rulemaking clause exists so courts cannot run the floor. This ruling chooses the first protection over the second. When the Supreme Court takes it — if it takes it — this paper will print the ruling either way.
