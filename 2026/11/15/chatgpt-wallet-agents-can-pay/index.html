ChatGPT Got a Wallet. Your Thumb Still Has to Sign Every Check.

OpenAI's new ChatGPT Wallet lets its "dots" agents finish a purchase for you, using a stand-in card number the agent never sees. For now, every single buy needs your Face ID or fingerprint.

By Herodotus·Sunday, November 15, 2026·Edition 61

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

For most of history, letting someone else spend your money meant handing over the purse, or at least the signet ring. OpenAI is trying a narrower trick: let the agent walk to the counter, but keep the purse in your pocket until you nod.

On Friday, November 13, OpenAI introduced ChatGPT Wallet, which lets its agents, called dots, complete purchases on a user's behalf. It starts with limited availability for ChatGPT Pro subscribers in the U.S., aged 18 and up, with eligible U.S.-issued cards. It was built with American Express, Mastercard and Visa. [1][2]

Here is how a purchase works, according to OpenAI. You add a card once. Before each purchase, you see the items, the total and the card, and you approve it with a security check such as Face ID or a fingerprint. Then the payment network issues a payment token, a substitute card number with its own security code and expiration date, so neither the agent nor the store sees your real card. OpenAI says card rewards and protections for eligible disputes carry over. [1]

OpenAI is open about the rough edges. Some websites may block agents, and stores' fraud systems may flag real agent purchases and decline them. The company published a "Commerce Partner Playbook" for businesses and named fraud firms Forter, Riskified, Sardine and Signifyd and payment companies Adyen, Checkout.com and Stripe as partners. [1] TechCrunch notes that Amazon, the largest retailer to ban AI agents, wants shoppers to use its own Alexa instead. [2]

The one-tap-per-purchase rule is not meant to last forever. OpenAI says it expects users eventually to set spending limits, categories or time windows and pre-approve purchases inside them. [1][2]

The timing is pointed. Earlier this week, United Airlines told AI agents they could look at its fares but not buy them. [3] OpenAI is now building the payment side of that fight.

Commentary. The wise part of this design is the friction. A purchase that needs your fingerprint is a purchase you agreed to, and a stand-in card number limits what can leak. The interesting moment comes later, when "pre-approved" arrives. Delegated power is useful precisely because it is limited, and that is as true of a shopping bot as of a government. Keep an eye on how wide those limits get, and who sets the defaults.
