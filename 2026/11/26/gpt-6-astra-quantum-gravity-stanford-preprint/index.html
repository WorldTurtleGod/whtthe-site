A Stanford Physicist Spent 400 Million AI Tokens on Quantum Gravity. The Paper Is Real. The Victory Laps Are Early.

Venkatesa Chandrasekaran posted a preprint to arXiv this week proposing a way to quantize Einstein's gravity, and he credits months of work with OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra for several of its calculations and proofs. Nobody has checked it yet, and the paper itself is more careful than the headlines.

A physicist at Stanford has put a new attempt at physics' hardest open problem on the public record, and an AI model helped write the math.

Venkatesa Chandrasekaran of Stanford's Leinweber Institute for Theoretical Physics is the sole author of "An algebraic quantization of gravity from the null cone." arXiv lists it as submitted November 25, 2026, at "54 pages + appendices, 34 figures." [1] OfficeChai called it an "80-plus-page research programme." That count likely includes the appendices. [3]

What it claims. Physics runs on two rulebooks that don't fit together. Quantum mechanics covers the very small, and Einstein's general relativity covers gravity and spacetime. Try to treat gravity the quantum way and the calculations blow up into infinities. [3] Chandrasekaran's abstract proposes "a background independent quantization of vacuum general relativity on four dimensional Lorentzian spacetimes." [1] In plain English, he describes gravity inside a region of spacetime using only data on the light cones at its edges, then applies the standard recipe for making a theory quantum, without assuming a fixed stage to start from. [1][3]

The paper reports several checks. It gets a finite, closed-form answer for gravitons being created and annihilated in pairs, where the usual method "yields a logarithmic divergence." It finds that a causal diamond of a given maximum size holds only a finite number of quantum states. It recovers the Bekenstein-Hawking entropy of black holes, matching at one loop a correction "Sen obtained through Euclidean methods." And it gets back Einstein's equations as the low-energy limit. [1] Those are serious hurdles, and most proposals never clear them.

Where the AI comes in. The paper's acknowledgements, as quoted by AI Frontier Post, say: "Several of the calculations and proofs in this paper were developed through detailed, back and forth collaboration with GPT-6 Astra over the course of many months, amounting to ~400M total tokens spent; I thank OpenAI for providing this compute." [2] Chandrasekaran also thanks OpenAI's Sam Altman and Sébastien Bubeck, according to both AI Frontier Post and OfficeChai. [2][3] OpenAI president Greg Brockman reposted the paper on X with the line "towards more deeply understanding the universe," AI Frontier Post reported. [2]

What it isn't. Social media got ahead of the paper. AI Frontier Post noted that Reddit threads billed it as an OpenAI paper and "physics' holy grail." It isn't an OpenAI paper. OpenAI provided compute, and the arXiv page lists no OpenAI author or affiliation. [2] The paper hasn't been peer reviewed. [2][3] It covers "vacuum" gravity only, meaning gravity with no matter in it. Its checks reproduce results physicists already had rather than predicting anything new that could be tested. And quantum-gravity effects show up at energies far beyond any collider. [2][3] OfficeChai flagged one more gap: the paper doesn't say which calculations the model did, so readers can't tell how much of the core idea came from the man and how much from the machine. [3]

This is a different kind of AI result from the Lean-checked math proofs of the past two weeks. A formal proof can be checked by computer in minutes. A physics theory has to survive other physicists, and eventually experiment. [2]

Commentary. The right response to this paper is the one science has always used: hand it to the people best qualified to break it and wait. The people cheering loudest have a stake. OpenAI supplied the compute, and its president is promoting the result. That doesn't make the physics wrong. It does mean the verdict should come from referees, not from reposts. Count it as a win when someone outside the building finds the theory holds, and not before.
