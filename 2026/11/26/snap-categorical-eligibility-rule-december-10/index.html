USDA Wants 6.3 Million People Off SNAP. The Public Has Until December 10 to Answer.

A rule the Agriculture Department published on November 10 would end "broad-based categorical eligibility," cutting federal SNAP spending by an estimated $44.6 billion over five years. Pennsylvania says 310,000 of its residents would lose benefits. Senate Democrats say Congress has refused to make this change by law more than once.

By Publius·Thursday, November 26, 2026·Edition 72

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Congress has declined at least three times to narrow food-stamp eligibility this way by law, according to a group of Senate Democrats. Now the Agriculture Department is doing it by regulation, and by its own count about 6.3 million people would lose benefits. [1][7]

The proposed rule, "Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program: Reforming Categorical Eligibility," ran in the Federal Register on November 10. Written comments are due December 10. [1][2] It targets a policy called broad-based categorical eligibility, or BBCE. Under current rules, a household that gets almost any benefit funded by Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF), including non-cash services like child care or job training, can count as automatically eligible for SNAP. The rule would limit that to households getting TANF "assistance" as defined at 45 CFR 260.31(a), which mostly means cash. [1][2]

USDA's own math: 3.2 million households with 6.3 million people would no longer qualify in fiscal 2029, the first year of full effect. Federal SNAP spending would fall by about $44.649 billion from fiscal 2027 through 2031. [1] States would spend about $928.5 million more on administration over those years, because caseworkers would go back to checking income limits and counting savings, the Federal Funds Information for States reported. [4]

USDA's case is that the program has drifted past what the law allows. The rule says BBCE "may threaten the integrity of the program by eroding public trust that SNAP benefits are available only to those who meet the eligibility limits set in law." [1][3] The rule puts a number on it: nearly $214 million in 2024 went to households over SNAP's statutory income or resource limits that still received a minimum benefit through BBCE. [1][3] For scale, the rule's five-year savings estimate is $44.6 billion. This is a rerun: Trump's first administration proposed a similar rule in 2019, and the Biden administration withdrew it in 2021. [3]

The fresh fight is in the states. On Wednesday, November 25, the Philadelphia Inquirer reported that Pennsylvania's Department of Human Services expects 310,000 Pennsylvanians, 52,000 of them in Philadelphia, to lose benefits worth more than $21 million. [5] "The Shapiro administration strongly opposes this cruel proposal," department spokesperson Brandon Cwalina told the paper. [5] According to the Inquirer, most recipients would lose eligibility once their savings reach $3,000 ($4,800 for seniors), and the rule would cap gross income at 130% of the poverty line, roughly $35,000 for a family of three, down from the 200% that states like Pennsylvania now allow. [5] The Greater Pittsburgh Community Food Bank, which is lobbying against the rule, puts the state's losses at 101,500 children and 87,000 older adults. [6]

On the Hill, eleven Senate Democrats, including John Fetterman of Pennsylvania, Michael Bennet, Amy Klobuchar and Cory Booker, wrote Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins demanding that she withdraw the rule. [7] They cite USDA's own analysis that nearly 1.8 million children would lose benefits. They also note that Congress considered restricting BBCE in the 2005 reconciliation debate and the 2014 and 2018 farm bills and dropped it every time. [7]

The sources don't agree on how many states use BBCE now. The rule counts 46 states. [1][2][3] The senators say 43 states plus D.C., Guam and the U.S. Virgin Islands. [7] FFIS says 44 states and territories. [4]

Commentary. A libertarian has no duty to defend every expansion of food stamps. The law sets income limits, and an agency enforcing those limits is doing its job. But Article I gives Congress the job of writing the rules, and by the senators' account Congress has considered this change three times and passed on it. Madison warned in Federalist No. 47 that piling the powers to make, carry out and judge the law into the same hands "may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny." A department rewriting eligibility for 6.3 million people on a 30-day comment clock, a week after the midterms as the Inquirer noted, is that warning in action. [5][8] And an asset test that punishes a working family for saving $3,000 treats thrift as a mistake. If the program is too big, cut it in the open, by recorded vote, where voters can see who did it.
