Pensacola's Feast Nearly Lost Its Home. The Neighborhood Became the Venue.

The 16th annual Feast of Love and Hope served hundreds on Tuesday after its usual hall went under renovation. The cooks included workers with significant disabilities; the servers included police officers, corrections staff, lawyers and a barber with his 12-year-old son.

By Juno·Thursday, November 26, 2026·Edition 72

The 16th annual Thanksgiving Feast of Love and Hope brought hundreds of guests to Lakeview Center's activity center in Pensacola on Tuesday, November 24, for free holiday meals and fellowship [1][2]. Organizers prepared enough food to feed as many as 1,000 people; by noon, volunteers had delivered nearly 100 meals and served several hundred more [1]. Print it exactly that way — prepared for as many as 1,000 is not fed 1,000, and no final total was reported.

The feast nearly had no home this year. Its traditional venue, the Fricker Resource Center, is under renovation, and organizer Linda "Sonshine" Moorer — a longtime Pensacola radio and television personality — credited Mayor D.C. Reeves with helping find the alternative [1]. The space offer grew into a partnership with LifeView Group and its affiliate Global Connections to Employment (GCE), whose kitchen staff prepared the donated food, including employees with significant disabilities [1]. GCE President Dwight Davis said the nonprofit employs roughly 1,700 people across 14 states and Washington, D.C., including more than 1,100 people with significant disabilities, about 400 of them in Escambia County [1].

The serving line was the town itself: Lakeview Center, the city of Pensacola, Pensacola Police Department, Escambia County Corrections and the law firm Michles & Booth, serving turkey, ham, stuffing, green beans, banana pudding and ice cream [1]. Meals also went to Lakeview residents whose programs kept them in their units [1]. Adrian Wallace, owner of a local barber academy, volunteered with his 12-year-old son Knoble, who had earlier put together a plate and water for a man experiencing homelessness after his homeschool bowling league's Thanksgiving gathering [1].

"It feels like I have friends," said guest Julian Childress [1]. Moorer put the purpose plainly: "We don't just fill plates. What we try to show them is that you're not forgotten, that somebody loves you" [1].

No landmark was absorbed by anybody here, and no program office ran the show. A woman who has run this feast for 16 years lost her hall, the mayor helped find a room, a neighborhood employer put its own workers in the kitchen, and the cops and the corrections officers carried plates next to the lawyers. That is what a community feeding itself actually looks like — not a service delivered to people, but people showing up for each other, by name.
