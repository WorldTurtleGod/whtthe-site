The City Cancelled Christmas. The Farm at the End of the Road Didn't.

Missy Sowell and her family have run the North Pole Christmas Light Display in Milton, Florida, for more than 30 years. Hurricane Isaias delayed this year's work and the city cancelled its own holiday events. The farm opened Friday night anyway — 7650 Sowell Road, nightly through Christmas Day.

Missy Sowell and her family have been working since August to prepare their 100-acre farm in Milton, Florida, for a month of entertaining visitors with the North Pole Christmas Light Display at Sowell Farms [1]. Sowell and her crew of four were still attending to last-minute decoration details on November 20, the day of opening, because preparations had been impacted by Hurricane Isaias, which hit Milton and the surrounding area on October 9 [1]. "We should have been done sooner," she said. "We're right up to the wire this year" [1]. The display opened its first evening Friday, November 20, and runs every evening through Christmas Day [1]. The farm's own announcement gives the event address as 7650 Sowell Rd, Milton, nightly 5:30–9 p.m. through December 25 [2].

The tradition launched more than 30 years ago, and it started the way family traditions do — small, and by hand. "I just started making little wooden pieces and putting lights on them," Sowell said. "And I started collecting" [1]. The collecting never stopped: "It's kind of turned into a sickness really," she said. "I don't stop buying. Things go on clearance and I start buying" [1]. The Pensacola News Journal reports more than two million lights would be twinkling by the time the gate opened at 5:30 p.m. — that figure is the Journal's, printed here as attributed [1]. This year the family purchased its fourth train to carry guests around the farm, this one from the New Orleans Zoo, and visitors can also drive through lighted tunnels; the display is never the same from year to year [1].

Who it is for matters more than the count. The display has become a popular place for assisted-care facilities to bring residents: "People that can't get out and walk around can see what all the other people see," Sowell said [1]. Santa and the Grinch appear, there is a separate food and merchandise vendor area, and there are camel rides [1]. The money, plainly: Sowell first asked only for donations to help keep the lights on; now, with a $6,000 power bill in the offing, the charge is $20 per vehicle and $3 for a train ride [1].

The fame belongs in its place — background, not the lede. Sowell Farms won a $50,000 prize as best of four competitors on "The Great Christmas Light Fight," aired December 16, 2019, and people have called her the Crazy Christmas Lady since [1]. Why this year matters in Milton: the city cancelled its holiday events following Hurricane Isaias, so Sowell expects a banner year [1]. "People tell me once they come over they want to come back," she said. "And Milton is growing" [1]. And on finding the place at all: "It's not like you're going to drive by us, we're at the end of the road" [1].

Her reason for doing it, in her own words: "Kids like lights," she said. "When I get finished with all the work and the kids start coming, that's what I love. I hear the squeal or laugh, that's what I love" [1].

Commentary: No institution runs this. A family of four on a 100-acre farm works from August, takes a hurricane in October, finishes "right up to the wire," and opens on Friday night because the tradition must go on — while the city itself cancelled Christmas. Two million lights by the Journal's count, twenty dollars a car against a six-thousand-dollar power bill, and a train for the folks who can't walk it. That is common-man culture with its sleeves rolled up.
