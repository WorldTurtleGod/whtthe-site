Did China Get America's Best AI Chips? "Yes," the Spy Chief Said on Sunday TV.

On CNN's State of the Union, Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton said China appears to have gotten access to restricted Nvidia chips, based on a pending New York indictment. Asked whether he had warned the Commerce secretary, he said no.

By Publius·Monday, November 23, 2026·Edition 69

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

For months the question has been whether America's export controls on top AI chips were leaking. On Sunday the country's top intelligence official answered it on live television.

The exchange. On CNN's State of the Union on Nov. 22, host Dana Bash asked Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton whether he could reassure Americans that China did not get access to Nvidia's advanced AI chips. [1] Clayton said Nvidia's top chips "are the coin of the realm. They are what everybody wants," and added: "I expect that there are channels through which China was able to get a hold of them." He pointed to a case his old office, the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Southern District of New York, brought against a company over efforts "to divert chips to China." [1]

Bash pressed: "And they were successful? China has these chips?" Clayton answered: "Yes, it appears, based on the facts alleged in the indictment, that those chips -- that there was access to those chips, yes." [1][2] He named no chip model and gave no count, and he limited the answer to what the indictment alleges. [2] Allegations in an indictment are not proven facts, and the case is pending. [1][3]

The question he dodged, and the one he didn't. Asked whether Alice Huang, the woman at the center of the scandal that ended Jensen Huang's run as Nvidia CEO, is a spy, Clayton declined. He said Attorney General Blanche has made clear the matter is under investigation, "both Ms. Huang and her company." [1] Bash noted that Jensen Huang is expected to testify before Congress next week. [1]

Then came the Commerce question. Bash read Senate Intelligence Chairman Tom Cotton's remark to The Wall Street Journal that action was "just a bit late, regrettably," and said Cotton had tried several times to warn Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick about companies suspected of diverting chips. Asked whether he had warned Lutnick in his current job, Clayton said: "No. And my current job is to provide information." [1] Asked whether Commerce made a mistake, he said he did not want to talk "in terms of mistake or not," and that America's "most exquisite technology should be reserved, to the greatest extent practical, for the United States." [1]

A Republican went further. On NBC's Meet the Press the same morning, Sen. John Kennedy, R-La., a member of the Senate Banking Committee, said it "appears" the Commerce Department "has allowed the Communist Party of China to circumvent our export controls on advanced chips." He added: "It appears that they knowingly allowed this to happen." Kennedy said he wants Lutnick and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent under oath before the committee. [4]

Where the accounts differ. Semafor reported that Clayton called Nvidia chips the "jewel… that everybody wants" on ABC's This Week. [5] The CNN transcript has him calling them "the coin of the realm" on CNN. [1] We quote the transcript. Hindustan Times, citing Clayton's CNN remarks, reported that the administration moved Nov. 17 to stop Chinese firms from remotely using Nvidia chips in data centers in third countries. [3]

Commentary. The Constitution gives Congress the power to regulate commerce with foreign nations. Congress handed the chip blacklist to the Commerce Department. When the nation's intelligence chief says on television that the controls leaked, and a Republican senator says the department "appears" to have let it happen knowingly, Congress is entitled to take back what it delegated. Hamilton argued in Federalist No. 70 that one of the virtues of a single executive is that the public can see clearly who is responsible when something goes wrong. So far no one in the administration has said on the record who should have caught it. Lutnick should answer under oath, on the record, before Congress changes hands Jan. 3.
