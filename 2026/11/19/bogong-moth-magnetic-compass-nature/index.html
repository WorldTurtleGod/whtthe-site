A Moth With a Brain a Tenth the Size of a Rice Grain Flies 1,000 Kilometres by Earth's Magnetic Field

Australia's Bogong moth already knew how to steer by the Milky Way. A Nature study published November 18 shows it has a second, independent compass — the tilt of Earth's magnetic field, the same trick night-migrating songbirds use — and loses its way when that field is cancelled, flipped, or stripped of violet-blue light.

The Bogong moth, Agrotis infusa, migrates up to 1,000 kilometres between breeding grounds across south-eastern Australia and a small number of cool caves in the Australian Alps — a journey each moth makes for the first time, with no prior visit and no guide [1][2]. Its brain is the size of one-tenth of a grain of rice [2].

A 2025 study showed the moths navigate by the stars. The new study, by Hui Chen, Eric Warrant and colleagues from Lund University and partner institutions, published in Nature on November 18, 2026, tests the backup system [1][2]. The paper's own summary: "Here we show that Bogong moths can use the Earth's magnetic field alone as a compass to navigate in their inherited migratory direction at night and that, similar to night-migratory songbirds, this compass is based on the field's inclination rather than its polarity" [1].

The moths were captured at Mount Selwyn, New South Wales, and flown tethered in a flight-simulator arena inside coils that could rotate, invert or cancel the geomagnetic field, under a randomised starry sky carrying no directional information [1][3]. Under the natural field, spring moths oriented south-west and autumn moths north-north-east, in their inherited seasonal directions [1]. Rotating the field 180 degrees reversed them almost exactly. Cancelling the field left them disoriented in both seasons [1][3]. Inverting polarity alone changed nothing; inverting inclination alone — the tilt — sent them the opposite way [1]. That is an inclination compass, the same principle night-migratory songbirds use [1][2].

Two more results narrow the mechanism. Removing ultraviolet, violet and blue light — leaving only wavelengths above 500 nanometres — disoriented the moths, and outdoor tests pinned the critical band to violet-blue, 380 to 500 nanometres [1]. Weak radio-frequency noise also disoriented them [1]. Both findings are consistent with a light-dependent radical-pair mechanism, possibly mediated by cryptochromes — and the paper keeps that at "consistent with" and "possibly," because the sensor itself has not been identified [1].

Warrant put the evolutionary point plainly: "It is remarkable that two such different groups of animals use the same basic principle to detect Earth's magnetic field. This suggests that very ancient biophysical mechanisms have been conserved through evolution" [2]. Chen: "What we see here is a navigational ability reminiscent of that found in migratory birds. Although insects and birds have evolved along separate paths for around 700 million years, they appear to be able to use similar biological solutions" [2].

The caveats ride with the wonder, as they should. Every core heading came from moths tethered in a five-minute flight simulator, not from moths tracked over the full migration [1]. The magnetic headings were noisier than the star-guided ones — the authors say the magnetic sense is inherently noisier than vision [1]. Which cryptochrome does the work, and how the moth combines stars, field and its other senses into one course, remain open questions the paper names itself [1]. Warrant: "The next step is to understand in greater detail how this mechanism works and how it interacts with other senses when animals navigate" [2].

A tenth of a grain of rice, carrying two compasses — one written in starlight, one in the tilt of the planet's field — and flying a thousand kilometres to a cave it has never seen. The instrument panel outperforms the brain-size jokes.
