We Didn't Just Outlive the Neanderthals — We Crowded Them Out

A new simulation study in Science Advances says climate change alone almost certainly didn't finish the Neanderthals. It was us — arriving, spreading, and cutting off the connections between their groups until the isolated bands couldn't recover.

By Juno·Tuesday, November 24, 2026·Edition 70

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

The last Neanderthals died out in Europe around the same time our own species, Homo sapiens, was spreading across the continent. A new study in the journal Science Advances, by Marco Vidal-Cordasco of the University of Cantabria in Spain and colleagues, used computer simulations to test whether those two events were connected — and its findings suggest modern humans may have been largely to blame [1][2].

The team built a computer model of prehistoric Europe, using past climate conditions and animal populations to estimate food availability between 55,000 and 27,000 years ago, then ran thousands of simulations comparing scenarios with and without Homo sapiens [1].

Climate alone, the model says, was not enough. "In most simulations, climate change alone wasn't enough to wipe out the Neanderthals. Although changing conditions reduced food supplies and caused populations to disappear from some regions, the Neanderthals survived in 98% of simulations when modern human populations remained small" [1]. Complete extinction happened in less than 2% of the climate-only simulations [3].

But when Homo sapiens entered the picture, the outcome changed sharply. "When simulations included the presence of H. sapiens and their populations sizes increased, the Neanderthal extinction risk increased remarkably," the researchers write [3]. The mechanism was not necessarily superior brains or better weapons. Growing human populations appear to have blocked Neanderthals from re-expanding into territory they had lost, leaving groups isolated and fragmented [1]. Populations that could move around and maintain contact with other groups tended to survive longer than more isolated ones. "Accordingly, connectivity between groups emerges as a critical factor influencing population stability and persistence," the team wrote in the paper [1]. "These findings support that Neanderthal disappearance and H. sapiens dispersals were interconnected demographic processes" [1].

One of the authors, Ana B. Marín-Arroyo, put the limits of the work plainly: "We don't have a time machine to go back 40,000 years, so there is always uncertainty. However, our results consistently show that demographic factors, especially population size, connectivity between groups, and repeated expansions of H. sapiens, had a much stronger effect on extinction risk than climate-driven changes in resource availability alone" [3].

Print the caveat in full, because it is the story: these were computer simulations, not definitive proof. "We can't point an accusing finger at Homo sapiens just yet," as the coverage notes [1]. What the study does is shift the weight of the evidence. For years the extinction debate ran on two rival explanations — a changing climate, or a smarter, better-armed us. This model says it may have been neither, or at least not in the way the argument was framed: not a war, and not the weather, but simple arithmetic. More of us, in more places, cutting the roads between their groups until each band stood alone. The Neanderthals did not necessarily lose a fight. They lost the map.
