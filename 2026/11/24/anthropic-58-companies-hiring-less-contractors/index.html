‘We May Not Be Firing, but We're Not Hiring.’ Anthropic Asked 58 of Its Customers What AI Is Doing to Jobs.

Only two of the companies said AI had fully eliminated a position. Two-thirds said it had cut their hiring, and nine had dropped contractors, many of them offshore. The study comes from the company selling the AI.

By Publius·Tuesday, November 24, 2026·Edition 70

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

The AI jobs story isn't mass layoffs, at least not yet. It's the jobs that never get posted.

On Monday, Nov. 23, Anthropic published "Working with AI," a study by its researchers Nathan Wilmers, Maxim Massenkoff and Peter McCrory based on 66 face-to-face interviews at 58 U.S. companies that use its Claude models. [1][2] The interviewees ran from structural engineers and dentists to hedge funds, law firms, a trucking company and a publisher. All are Anthropic customers. The authors say plainly that the sample is "not a random or systematically representative sample." [1]

The headline finding is about hiring, not firing. Anthropic wrote that it saw "very few examples of domestic layoffs that were driven by AI automation." People at only two companies had seen AI automation fully eliminate a position and cost someone their job. People at five companies described pushing out employees who would not adopt AI. Ten companies said they were hiring more because of AI, mostly into AI implementation and IT support. [1][2]

But people at two-thirds of the companies said AI had reduced their hiring. "We may not be firing, but we're not hiring," one told the researchers. A third of those declines came from shrinking teams through attrition and not refilling the jobs. Most came from companies skipping hires they would otherwise have needed to grow. "We've added a lot of capacity by buying tokens instead of hiring people," an engineering leader at a health management company said. [1]

The sharpest cuts landed outside the company walls. Nine companies described cutting contracted software developers, customer service representatives, legal counsel and back-office staff, "many of which they had already offshored." One finance company downsized "well over 100 offshore data and developer contractors," and a software company said its "legal bill has just completely disappeared" after AI contract reviews. One firm that built a document-review agent cut the number of reviewers it expected to hire next year by more than 90%. The authors call the contractor cuts a possible "early warning of job disruption in the international business services industry." [1]

What the AI is mostly doing is writing code. Interviewees described 100 pieces of AI-written software in enough detail to sort them. Eighty percent automated work people had done by hand, and 20% replaced software the companies had been buying from vendors. [1][2] The bottleneck has moved. AI Weekly highlighted the report's finding that "code review became a critical bottleneck." [2] Anthropic also lists experts resisting deployment and tasks AI can't speed up, like sales and strategic decisions. [1]

Anthropic's conclusion is that the biggest payoffs came from companies that reorganized, broadening jobs and moving people to "bottleneck" tasks, rather than bolting AI onto the old org chart. It adds that this "will not replace a strong policy response in the event of substantial technological disruption." [1]

Commentary. Read this for what it is: a vendor interviewing its own customers about its own product. That doesn't make it wrong, and the authors are upfront about the sample. Still, the most honest numbers in it are the uncomfortable ones. Two-thirds hiring less. Offshore contractors cut by the hundred. Those effects never show up in a layoff count, and they will hit entry-level American workers first, as the jobs they would have started in quietly disappear. Before Washington reaches for a "strong policy response," it should get its own data instead of waiting on the companies selling the tools to tell it what's happening.
