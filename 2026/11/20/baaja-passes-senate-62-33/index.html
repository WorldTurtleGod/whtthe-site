62 to 33: The Permitting Bill Passed the Senate — Softer at Every Step, and Through Anyway

Vote 270 is in the official record: H.R. 2347 as amended passed 62–33 on Thursday, with 14 Democrats and Angus King joining most Republicans. Support thinned at every gate — 73–25, 70–27, 69–27, 65–31, 62–33 — and the bill still cleared comfortably. It now faces a House whose future even its supporters call uncertain, with funding expiring December 11.

Final passage happened, and this time the number comes from the Senate's own roll call, not a preview. Vote 270, on Thursday, November 19: H.R. 2347, as amended — the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act — passed 62–33, with five senators not voting [1][2]. Reuters, reporting the same tally, calls it a sweeping bill "to speed approvals for large projects such as power plants, transmission lines, mines and data centers," and notes it "now heads to the Republican-controlled House of Representatives. If it passes, President Donald Trump has said he will sign the bill into law" [3].

The split, from the official record: 14 Democrats voted yes, along with independent Angus King of Maine; the Republican noes were Josh Hawley of Missouri and Mike Rounds of South Dakota, with independent Bernie Sanders also voting no [2][4]. The five not voting were Bill Cassidy, John Cornyn, Mitch McConnell and Tommy Tuberville, all Republicans, and Democrat Chris Murphy [2].

Read the flips against Wednesday's cloture vote, because they cut both ways and this paper prints both. Kirsten Gillibrand of New York and Raphael Warnock of Georgia voted to end debate and then voted no on the bill itself [2][5]. Gary Peters of Michigan did the reverse — no on cloture, yes on passage [2][5]. Amy Klobuchar of Minnesota, who missed the cloture vote, voted yes; Ron Johnson of Wisconsin, also absent Wednesday, voted yes; Adam Schiff of California, absent Wednesday, voted no [2][5]. Four Republicans who voted yes on cloture — Cassidy, Cornyn, McConnell and Tuberville — did not vote on passage [2][5].

The trend line is the honest frame: 73–25 to take the bill up, 70–27 to stop debating the substitute, 69–27 to adopt it, 65–31 to end debate on the bill, 62–33 to pass it [6]. At every single step, the yes column got smaller. It never got close to failing. Co-author Shelley Moore Capito of West Virginia claimed the win plainly: "Tonight, the passage of permitting reform in the Senate finally became reality and we took a major step towards addressing the affordability concerns of the American people" [3]. Senator Jeff Merkley of Oregon, who fought the bill's tribal and states'-rights provisions to the end, answered from the floor before the vote: "So this is where we are: a bill that was dropped as we were leaving for elections that touches on multiple incredibly important factors for states' rights and tribal rights and energy," adding, "That needs to be thoughtfully considered in 2027" [3].

What did not change on the way through: no roll call adopted S.Amdt. 7022, the authors' late tribal-consultation fix, and this paper does not print its language as part of the bill [6]. The survivor-relief measure that gave the vehicle its name still rides as Division C — passed, not emptied [3][6].

Now the clock, and it is not friendly. USA Today reports the bill "faces an uncertain future in the House of Representatives and a shrinking legislative window," and that if both chambers do not adopt it, "the legislation would have to be reintroduced when the 120th Congress takes over in January" [7]. The Wall Street Journal adds that in the House, "some Democrats have voiced opposition and many Republicans are bracing to leave Capitol Hill after losing their seats in the midterms" [4]. Federal funding expires December 11, and the 119th Congress ends January 3, 2027 [6][7].

Commentary: Score it the way this paper has scored it all week, same standard for both parties. A permitting regime that averages years of delay is power exercised by process, and the Senate just voted — bipartisan, in the open, on the record — to cut it. That is the win, and it is real. The cost side of the ledger is also real: the tribal-consultation fix never got its vote, and the bill now goes to a House racing a funding deadline and the end of a Congress, where must-pass pressure is exactly how provisions nobody voted on get traded away. Who pays, and whose say gets traded — the Senate answered the first half. Watch the House for the second.
