A Bar That Was "Helen's Living Room" Came Home to Family — and a Packed Room

The Brass Rail in Port Huron, Michigan, founded in 1937 when Helen David turned her family's ice cream parlor into a bar, reopened Wednesday after nearly a year of renovations — bought back into family ownership, restored by hand, and full fifteen minutes after the doors opened.

By Juno·Friday, November 20, 2026·Edition 66

Share

Just 15 minutes after opening its doors at 1 p.m. on Wednesday, November 18, the Brass Rail was already bustling — customers filling the bar and the booths, reconnecting with familiar faces, celebrating the return of a downtown Port Huron landmark [1]. The historic bar at 410 Huron Ave. has reopened after nearly a year of renovations, this time with plans to stay open year-round [1].

The Brass Rail began on June 15, 1937, when Helen David, with her mother and her aunt Mary McLoof, transformed the family's ice cream parlor — Hibye's, run by Lebanese immigrants Tony and Elizabeth Hibye — into a bar [2]. Its longtime motto: "Where all good people meet" [2]. In late 2025, Tony Abou-Ganim — a relative of David who poured his first drink there in 1980 under her tutelage, and who collected empty bottles in its basement as a boy in exchange for Shirley Temples — partnered with local developer Jimmy Radatz to purchase and restore it, returning the business to family ownership [1][2]. Abou-Ganim did not give a renovation figure, but said he has invested much of his retirement savings into the project, hoping to preserve the Brass Rail for another 90 years [2].

They reopened temporarily for the 2025 holiday season, then closed in early January for the real work, aiming at June — the 89th anniversary of the founding [1][2]. The building had other plans: crews found electrical, plumbing and heating and cooling problems, plus asbestos removal and fire-suppression work [1][2]. What they kept is the point of the story: the original wood floors restored, the ornate wooden bar refinished, decorative tin ceilings uncovered after years hidden, the familiar red-and-black booths still in place, and an expansion into 412 Huron Ave. for restrooms and a cocktail prep area [1]. Radatz: "This whole building is a time capsule, and I hope that people see how special it is" [1]. Abou-Ganim, the morning after: "It's just a dream come true" [1].

The people in the room explain why a bar gets two newspaper stories in two days. Jeffrey Koopman, a longtime customer who remembers coming with his grandfather between Port Huron Flags hockey periods in the late 1960s and early 1970s, volunteered on the demolition crew — tearing out flooring and parts of the old bar while preserving its fixtures [1]. "It was pretty cathartic to be able to go through a place that I cared so much about and see it coming back to what it was meant to be," he said; asked how walking in Wednesday felt, his answer was one word: "A relief" [1]. Sue Bannheyne, wife of former Flags captain Jim Bannheyne, remembered arriving in town with no family nearby: "We had no family close by, and she became family to us, so coming in here was like coming to Helen's living room" [1]. Bethany Belanger, 80, of Port Huron: "It's family. It's friends. It is the most welcoming place" [1]. And: "It's fond memories. We're going to have more" [1].

The signature Tom and Jerry cocktails are back — traditional, oatmeal cookie and Thin Mint varieties, Helen David's own recipe tradition, at $12 — alongside Uncle Charlie's Old Fashioned and nonalcoholic cocktails [1][2]. The bar now runs Wednesday through Sunday, 1 p.m. to about 11 p.m., with a ribbon-cutting held Thursday, November 19, at 12:45 p.m. with the Blue Water Area Chamber of Commerce [1][2]. Bartender Tamy Middleton, who worked the Rail from 2010 to 2016 and came back when she heard it was returning to Helen David's family, was asked what has stayed the same. "It's a family," she said. "I want them to feel like they just left their friend's living room or a family member's living room" [2].

Commentary: No program absorbed this landmark and no organisation took it over. A man put his retirement savings into the bar where he collected bottles as a boy, a local developer partnered with him, a customer picked up a demolition tool for a place he loved, and the town showed up fifteen minutes after the doors opened. That is common-man culture doing what it has always done — keeping its own places alive with its own hands and its own money.
