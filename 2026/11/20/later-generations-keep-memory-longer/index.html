Your Grandparents Forgot Faster. A Study of 219,000 People Says Your Memory Will Last Longer.

People born later in the 20th century reach old age with sharper memories and lose them more slowly, according to a Nature Medicine study led from Oslo. The gain may be big enough to explain why dementia rates have been falling for decades.

By Publius·Friday, November 20, 2026·Edition 66

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Here is some good news that nobody had to vote for. Americans and Europeans born later in the last century are reaching old age with better memories than their parents had, and they are holding on to them longer.

That is the finding of a study published Nov. 17 in Nature Medicine and written up by Nature's news desk on Nov. 19. Researchers led by Anders Fjell and Kristine Walhovd at the University of Oslo analyzed about 783,000 memory tests taken by about 219,000 people aged 50 and older, born between 1910 and 1964, in five long-running studies in Europe and the United States, some followed for up to 30 years. [1][2][3]

The test is simple: hear a list of words, then recall as many as you can. Doctors use the same kind of test to screen for dementia and Alzheimer's disease. [2]

The authors call the result a "double dividend." Later-born generations start old age with higher memory scores and decline more slowly afterward. [1] Nature reported that a 10-year jump in birth date was tied to a 10% higher memory score at age 60, and that with each later birth decade, people were 64% more likely to keep a stable memory with little or no decline. [2] ScienceBlog, working from the paper and an earlier open-access version, reported that each decade of later birth was worth about 0.54 more recalled words and a yearly decline 0.070 words shallower. [3]

The gains were not spread evenly. They were concentrated among the people whose memories fell fastest, the group most at risk. [1] ScienceBlog reported the generational effect among the fastest decliners was about twice the size of the effect for a typical person. [3]

The brain scans line up with the word lists. The team looked at imaging data from nearly 2,000 people over a median of four years and found that stable memory went with less shrinkage of the hippocampus, the brain region that forms memories. [2] According to ScienceBlog, severe decliners lost hippocampal volume at 2% a year, against 0.63% a year for people whose memory held flat. [3]

Why it matters: dementia risk at a given age has been dropping. Nature cited a 2020 analysis finding a 13% drop per decade in Europe and the United States, and a 2025 JAMA paper finding an individual's risk of dementia at any given age has fallen by two-thirds over 40 years in the U.S. [2] The paper's abstract says the combined memory advantage is "large enough to plausibly account for" that 13% decline, and that calendar-time trends hitting all generations at once are "unlikely" to explain the differences. [1]

"For those who reach 80, a smaller percentage [of people] will get dementia than before," Fjell told Nature. Carol Brayne, a Cambridge dementia epidemiologist not on the study, called the findings "confirmatory" and "another piece of the jigsaw." [2]

Now the fine print. The study is observational. It does not show what caused the gains. Word recall is not a dementia diagnosis. Untangling birth-year effects from age and calendar effects rests on assumptions, and the people who declined fastest may have dropped out before taking four tests, ScienceBlog noted. The data come only from Europe and the U.S. [3] The authors argue that memory level is built mainly in early life and the rate of decline is shaped by adult conditions, and they call for prevention "across the whole lifespan, beginning in childhood." [1] ScienceBlog reported the earlier version named better control of vascular risk, less smoking and lower pollution as plausible adult factors. The study did not test them. [3]

Commentary. The likely suspects are more schooling, better childhood health and fewer cigarettes, though the paper doesn't prove any of them. If they are the cause, most of that was built by families and ordinary people making better choices over a century, not by any one agency. The paper is honest that it cannot name causes. That should be a warning to any politician who wants to claim credit for falling dementia rates or spend big on a single fix. Show the evidence first, then write the check.
