The Biggest IPO in History Carried a Warning About the End of the World

Anthropic priced above its range, sold 980 million shares at $83, and raised a record $81 billion on Thursday at a $1.5 trillion valuation — while its own prospectus warned of "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity," and its CEO used the Nasdaq podium to promise he would not bend.

Anthropic went public on Thursday, November 19, and the numbers need no adjectives. The Claude maker sold 980 million shares at $83 each, pricing above its initial $70-to-$78 range, in an offering Reuters reports raised $81.3 billion — the largest stock-market debut on record [1][2]. An additional $26 billion private placement takes the total haul to over $107 billion, at a valuation of about $1.5 trillion [1]. Shares opened at $111.50, 34 percent above the IPO price, and closed at $97.17, up 17 percent, putting the market value at about $1.3 trillion on outstanding shares and $1.8 trillion fully diluted [2][3]. The ticker is ANT, on the Nasdaq [2].

Reuters frames what the listing means for the industry: Anthropic is the first frontier AI company to go public, "poised to become the sector's benchmark," with rival OpenAI reportedly eyeing a 2027 listing [1]. Every AI valuation argument from here on has a public price to point at — including the argument against. Anthropic carries nearly $900 billion in computing commitments with partners including Amazon and Google, who together hold about 28.4 percent of the company [1][3]. That is the bubble question in one line: an enormous buildout, financed in a circle of partners who are also shareholders, that pays off only if the returns arrive.

And then there is the filing. In its own IPO prospectus, Anthropic warned that AI could behave unpredictably and advance faster than oversight mechanisms, posing potentially "catastrophic or existential risks to humanity" [1]. Read that again with the price tag attached: the company that just sold $81 billion of stock told its buyers, in the legal document, that its product category might end the world. Whether you read that as honesty or as liability armor, no prospectus language like it has ever carried a debut this size.

CEO Dario Amodei used the bell podium the way he has used every podium. "We will not bend to either business nor political pressure," Amodei said at the Nasdaq on Thursday in New York. "Today puts the company in a stronger financial position, and in doing so only strengthens our resolve to do the right thing" [2]. The background Reuters supplies: Amodei clashed with the Pentagon and the Trump administration over military use of Anthropic's models for mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons, rejected the contract terms, and challenged a government blacklist in court [2]. Last month, answering a post on X that challenged the company's safety record, he said Anthropic had "likely sacrificed a significant chunk of a several-month model lead" by delaying its Mythos models over hacking risks [2].

The skeptics get their lines too, because the price is now public property. Jake Behan, head of capital markets at Direxion: "I've looked at this whole week as a referendum on AI. Nvidia gave us a read on the buildout, and Anthropic is about to give us a read on investor appetite for the next generation of AI companies" [1]. Nic Puckrin, cross-asset analyst and founder of Coin Bureau, put the buyer's warning in one sentence: "It doesn't have to be a bad company for it to be a bad investment at the IPO price" [1].

Commentary: Two questions, applied the way this paper applies them. Who pays? For once, the answer is volunteers — public investors who read a prospectus that warned them, in writing, about existential risk, and paid $83 a share anyway. That is a free market working with its eyes open, and it beats the alternative of the same buildout financed quietly on debt nobody voted for. What does it cost in freedom? Watch the other half of Amodei's sentence. A newly public company with $900 billion in commitments and quarterly shareholders to answer to has promised not to bend to business pressure. The market will test that promise every quarter, in public, starting now — and for the first time, everyone can watch the test happen.
