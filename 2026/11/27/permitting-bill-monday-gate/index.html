Congress Comes Back Monday. The Permitting Bill Is First Through the Gate.

The House Rules Committee meets Monday afternoon on the Senate-passed permitting bill, and the groups trying to kill it say this is their last stop.

By Juno·Friday, November 27, 2026·Edition 73

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Congress is still out for Thanksgiving, but Monday's first move is already on the calendar. The House Committee on Rules will meet Monday, November 30, 2026 at 4:00 PM ET in H-313 on the Senate amendment to H.R. 2347, the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act of 2026 [1]. No House vote exists yet. What exists is a scheduled gate, and the bill has to get through it.

The Senate passed the bill 62-33 last week, sending a sweeping permitting package to the Republican-controlled House to speed approvals for large projects such as power plants, transmission lines, mines and data centers [2]. President Donald Trump has said he will sign it if it passes [2].

The opposition is treating the recess as a countdown. Environmental groups are scrambling to convince on-the-fence House Democrats to kill the bill, leaning on growing Democratic opposition, a packed calendar, and Republican absences when Congress returns [3]. One environmental lobbyist put the stakes plainly: "It's the last stop before it goes to Trump’s desk" [3].

Attendance is part of the fight. Speaker Mike Johnson was forced to postpone a vote last week because there weren't enough members in town, then issued a letter Friday asking Republicans to return to Washington after the holiday [3]. Punchbowl News reported Tuesday evening that leadership was planning to bring the bill forward under a rule, requiring a simple majority vote to pass [3]. That is reported, not announced. Politico reported some Democrats are seeking an open amendment process, which if successful would send the legislation back to the Senate with three weeks left on the legislative calendar [3].

The take: Washington built the permitting maze, and now Washington offers a faster route through its own maze. Shorter federal reviews are better than endless ones. But a permission slip issued faster is still a permission slip, and the same federal gatekeepers keep the gate. Who pays when a project waits years for stamps? The people who buy the power. What freedom does the wait cost? The freedom to build without asking first. Monday's Rules meeting is where the House decides how much of that gate it is willing to open.
