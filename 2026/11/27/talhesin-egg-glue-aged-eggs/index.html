Scientists Built a Replacement Glue for Aging Eggs. It Works in Mice. Humans Are Years Away.

A Max Planck team engineered "TALhesin," a protein system that stands in for the chromosome glue eggs lose with age. In 17-month-old mice it lifted the share of eggs with no premature splitting from 46.7% to 74.1%, about what young mice show. Outside experts call it clever and warn nobody has fertilized a treated egg yet.

By Publius·Friday, November 27, 2026·Edition 73

One of the biggest reasons fertility drops after 35 is a slow failure of glue. A team in Germany says it has built a substitute.

In a paper published in Science on Thursday, November 26, researchers led by biochemist Melina Schuh at the Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences in Göttingen described an artificial cohesion system called TALhesin. [1][2][3]

The problem. Each chromosome in an egg is two identical halves, chromatids, held together by a protein complex called cohesin. Eggs form before birth, so by a woman's late 30s they are decades old, and cohesin slowly falls off and is never replaced. Chromatids then split too early and get sorted wrong, leaving eggs with too many or too few chromosomes. [2][3] That condition, aneuploidy, is a cause of infertility, failed IVF and early pregnancy loss, Nature reported. [2]

The fix. TALhesin uses programmable DNA-binding proteins (TAL effectors) aimed at the region near the centromere where cohesin normally holds the halves together. Two of them lock onto each other, acting as an engineered tether. [3] The team also built in a cut site for separase, the egg's own "molecular scissor," so the tether lets go at the right moment of cell division. [1][3]

The numbers. Researchers injected messenger RNA for the proteins into immature mouse eggs. [2] In 17-month-old mice, the share of eggs with no premature chromatid separation rose from 46.7% to 74.1%, close to the 74.7% seen in young mice. [2][3] They also adapted the system to human chromosomes: in donated, unfertilized eggs from clinics in Göttingen, Berlin and Cambridgeshire, chromosomes carrying more TALhesin had their halves held closer together. [2][3] "With TALhesin, we can replace lost chromatid cohesion for the first time," Schuh said in the institute's statement. [3]

The caveats, from people not on the paper. Bettina Mihalas, a reproductive biologist at the University of New South Wales, told Nature it is "a very creative, well-engineered and well-thought-out approach." [2] But several experts quoted by the UK's Science Media Centre pressed hard on what wasn't tested. [4] Alastair Sutcliffe of University College London noted "No eggs were fertilised, no embryos were produced, no embryo was karyotyped, and no mouse was born." [4] He said the 47%-to-74% headline comes from one age group at one dose, and the effect was smaller in 20-month-old mice. He also warned that too much of the tether caused the very errors it is meant to prevent. [4] Alison Campbell of Care Fertility called it "an elegant proof of principle" but said the human egg numbers are small and patients "should not expect to see these technologies in IVF clinics for some years." [4] The Science Media Centre notes Campbell advises a company working on the same problem by a different route. [4]

Schuh agrees the road is long: "further research and safety testing will be needed before clinical use." [3] The team's next step is testing eggs donated by older women. [3]

Commentary. This is good, honest basic science. The researchers call it proof of principle, and the outside experts are right to hold them to that. IVF is expensive, and couples paying out of pocket are exactly who gets sold hope early. Read the fine print: mouse eggs, scored under a microscope, no babies yet.
