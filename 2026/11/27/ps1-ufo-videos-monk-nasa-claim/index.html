A Monk Says He Hid NASA's UFO Footage in a 1996 PlayStation Game. Someone Cracked the Disc.

On a podcast this week, a Buddhist priest who runs a space museum in Hakui, Japan, said he copied raw UFO film at NASA's Johnson Space Center and tucked it into a PS1 game as bonus content. Within days a Redditor pulled 90 videos off the disc with Linux and a script written by Claude. Whether any of it came from NASA is unproven.

By Publius·Friday, November 27, 2026·Edition 73

Some people hide secrets in a safe. Josen Takano says he hid his on a PlayStation disc.

Takano, a Buddhist monk and curator of a space and UFO museum in Hakui, on Japan's Sea of Japan coast, sat down with Jesse Michels for the American Alchemy podcast in an episode posted November 24. [1][2] His story, as summarized by UAP Trail, a site that catalogs UFO podcasts: NASA staff let him into a building at the Johnson Space Center in Houston that he calls "Omega," where he opened drawers of raw UFO footage, had the originals copied and sent them back. [2] Cybernews reported he said he traveled from Houston to Los Angeles to duplicate the material, and that he no longer has the footage himself. [3]

Where did it go? Into a video game, he says. Takano told Michels the footage played as bonus material when you finished the game, and described it as "fireflies floating around" with "bogeys" tailing along, according to Cybernews. [3] Michels tracked the game down as the 1996 PlayStation title Junichi Yaoi: Top Secret Project: Chase the UFO. [3] Did NASA know? "Oh, I didn't tell them," Takano said, per Cybernews. [3]

Then Michels threw down a challenge: "If you're a VFX expert, video analyst, gamer, UFO nerd, or just someone who loves internet mysteries, go look at this thing, analyze the footage, and let me know what you think." [3]

The internet answered within days. A Reddit user found the game's disc image, took it apart on Linux, and had Anthropic's Claude write a Python script to fix mismatched frame sizes, put the sequences back in order and sync the audio, Cybernews reported. [3] Out came 90 videos, posted to YouTube in three parts. They range from grainy space shots and a glowing diamond-shaped object to what the uploader called "genuinely horrible footage of some sort of foil structure that appears to be smoking." [3] Some Redditors said the clips had been floating around for years. [3]

What's proven: not much. The disc is real. A fan's translation of its manual on GameFAQs says it came out May 24, 1996, and describes it as a UFO "database package" more than a game. It also quotes the box promising "496 top secret photos and 33 video from around the world, including unreleased photos and films from NASA." [4] So the NASA claim was printed on the box 30 years ago. The count doesn't match, though: 33 videos on the box, 90 pulled off the disc. [3][4] The rest rests on one man's memory from more than 30 years ago. No NASA record ties any clip to the agency. Cybernews is so far the only news outlet to report the extraction itself. [3] Even the host is checking: Michels ran a reverse image search on a photo Takano says astronaut Gene Cernan gave him, supposedly showing a "construction" on the far side of the Moon. It turned out to be an aerial view of the Palace of Ardashir in Iran, UAP Trail reported. [2]

The episode's YouTube title calls Takano "BANNED FROM THE U.S." [1] Cybernews reported Takano said he was stopped at the airport and told he wasn't allowed to fly home; we could not find any record confirming it. [3]

Commentary. The fun part is real either way. A decades-old Japanese disc got cracked open by one guy at home with free tools and an AI helper, in the time it takes a federal agency to acknowledge a records request. If NASA wants to settle it, the agency can say whether these clips came out of its files. Until then: great story, zero proof.
