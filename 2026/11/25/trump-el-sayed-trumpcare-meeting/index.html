The President Who Campaigned Against Medicare for All Now Wants a Meeting About It

President Trump told the New York Post he will meet Michigan Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed about the Democrat's Medicare for All pitch — rebranded "Trumpcare." He committed to nothing. But a Republican president entertaining government coverage for everyone deserves the same two questions this paper asks of any growth of government.

By Juno·Wednesday, November 25, 2026·Edition 71

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

President Donald Trump said Tuesday, November 24, that he plans to meet with Michigan Sen.-elect Abdul El-Sayed to discuss the Democrat's "Medicare for All" proposal, which El-Sayed has pitched to rebrand as "Trumpcare" [1]. The remarks came in a 45-minute interview with the New York Post in the Oval Office [1].

"He's a smart guy," Trump told the Post of El-Sayed's suggestion to rename the plan after him. "He did a good job. He actually did a very good job" [1]. "I know I'll be meeting with him," Trump continued. "Maybe there's some place in the middle that we could go" [1].

El-Sayed made universal health care central to his campaign. Earlier this month he posted an image of a gold "Trumpcare" card on X, calling on the president to guarantee health care "from cradle to grave," and Trump reposted a screenshot of the post on Saturday [1]. El-Sayed has said he would meet with Trump or anyone else who could advance his goals. "I am happy to meet with him, like I'll meet with anyone who can advance the goals I ran on to get money out of politics, put money in pockets, pass Medicare for all," El-Sayed said on ABC's "This Week" [1].

Trump paired the openness with a warning about the incoming Democratic majorities. "I'm just going to veto everything there is, and then at some point I think they're going to want to negotiate. I will, and we can do health care. We can do a lot of great things together," Trump said [1].

Grade it as it sits: the stated plan to meet, and the quoted remarks, are documented. There is no bill, no terms, and no commitment to single-payer health care in the reporting — Trump suggested possible middle ground without saying what it would contain [1]. The turnaround from a bitter midterm campaign, in which the two traded personal attacks, is also on the record [1].

Commentary: Hold both parties to the same standard. If a Democratic president sat down to discuss government-backed coverage for every American, this paper would ask who pays for it and what freedom it costs. A Republican president entertaining the same conversation gets the same questions, not a pass because his name might go on the card. Who pays? Any version of guaranteed coverage "from cradle to grave" is paid for by taxpayers and by everyone whose premiums and choices get folded into a government system. What freedom does it cost? The freedom to choose your own coverage — and your own doctor — without Washington in the room. None of that is decided by one Oval Office interview. But the door is open, and readers should watch what walks through it.
