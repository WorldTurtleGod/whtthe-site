The Same Charge Jack Smith Used on Trump Is Now Being Waved at Democrats. No One Has Been Charged.

A Texas U.S. attorney and the White House counsel have warned incoming Democratic investigators that their oversight could amount to a federal "conspiracy against rights." Legal experts call it payback; the Justice Department says it is enforcing the law.

By Publius·Wednesday, November 25, 2026·Edition 71

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

The Trump administration is warning the Democrats about to take over congressional committees that investigating the president's allies could be a federal crime. The law it keeps citing is the same one special counsel Jack Smith used against Donald Trump in 2023.

That law is 18 U.S.C. 241, the Civil War-era "conspiracy against rights" statute. It makes it a crime for two or more people to conspire to "injure, oppress, threaten, or intimidate" anyone in the free exercise of a right secured by the Constitution or federal law. [1] Politico reported Tuesday that White House and Justice Department officials have spent recent days pointing Democrats toward it. [2]

The man doing most of the pointing is Aaron Reitz, U.S. attorney for the Southern District of Texas. According to the Washington Times, Reitz has sent letters to Sens. Richard Blumenthal, Elissa Slotkin, Adam Schiff and Elizabeth Warren and to Reps. Jamie Raskin and Robert Garcia, citing Sections 241 and 242 and ordering them to preserve records. [3] Politico counted letters to seven Democratic lawmakers. [2] In the letter to Schiff, Reitz demanded records about what he called "your intent to weaponize Congressional investigations and deprive citizens of their core constitutional rights." [2] The Times reported he accused Blumenthal of violating the right of assembly by proposing to investigate donors to Trump's White House ballroom. [3]

Reitz has acknowledged no formal criminal investigation has begun, the Times reported, and Attorney General Todd Blanche said Nov. 12 that the department has no criminal investigations about statements by incoming lawmakers. [3] Reitz's office covers southern Texas; the lawmakers he is writing to work in Washington. [3]

The White House joined in. Counsel Will Scharf wrote to Garcia, who is in line to chair the House Oversight Committee: "Issuing unlawful subpoenas with the intention of depriving citizens of their constitutional rights and privileges may warrant further investigation." [2][3] The Times also reported a one-page Scharf memo requiring agencies to get White House approval before any employee agrees to testify before Congress. [3]

The Justice Department defended the approach to Politico: "Oversight is not a license to threaten private citizens for exercising their First Amendment rights." [2] Critics were blunt. "Given it's such a stretch, I'd look at it more as just political payback," Capital University law professor Mark Brown told Politico. [2] The watchdog group Campaign for Accountability has filed a State Bar of Texas ethics complaint against Reitz, according to the Times. [3]

Both sides have stretched this statute before. Politico noted that Biden's Justice Department used it to convict meme-poster Douglass Mackey, a conviction an appeals court later overturned, and that Smith's use of it against Trump was itself called novel. [2]

Commentary. Madison wrote in Federalist No. 51 that "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" — each branch checks the others. [4] Congress's power to investigate the executive is one of those checks. An executive-branch prosecutor threatening the legislators who will oversee his bosses, while the White House gates who may testify, turns the check upside down. If a Democratic subpoena ever truly breaks the law, courts can quash it. Mailing criminal-statute warnings before a single subpoena is issued is intimidation of the very kind Section 241 was written to punish. Both parties have now bent this law into a weapon. The next majority will use it too.
