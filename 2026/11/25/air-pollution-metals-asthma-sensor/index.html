Metal Dust in Dirty Air Flips a Switch in Your Airways. Scientists Say Existing Drugs Can Flip It Back.

A King's College London and Cardiff University study links metal particles in air pollution to severe asthma attacks and names the airway sensor they trigger. Drugs that block it worked in lab tests, not yet in patients.

By Publius·Wednesday, November 25, 2026·Edition 71

Share

Copied Share on X Facebook Reddit Email

Doctors have known for decades that dirty air makes asthma worse. A new study says it has found how, and a possible way to stop it.

Researchers led by King's College London and Cardiff University reported that metal particles in air pollution trigger changes in the airways that cause inflammation and contribute to asthma attacks and chronic bronchitis, according to a King's College release published November 24. [1] The work appears in The Lancet's eBioMedicine journal, and the university calls it the first to identify the biological processes linking pollution exposure to lung disease. [1]

The study came at the problem two ways, according to the university. [1]

First, the numbers. Working with scientists at Imperial College London and Queen Mary University of London, the team analyzed health and environmental data from a large population over time and found a link between exposure to metals in air pollution and severe asthma attacks. [1] That suggests tracking the metal content of the air, not just the overall soot level, could warn people with asthma of "bad asthma days." [1] The release does not give the size of the population or the size of the effect.

Second, the mechanism. The researchers studied a sensor on airway cells called the calcium-sensing receptor, or CaSR. Using human lung tissue and cells, they found that pollution particles switch the CaSR on, which triggers inflammation and makes the airways more sensitive and more prone to narrowing. [1] Existing drugs that block the CaSR prevented those harmful effects in laboratory studies. [1]

That last part matters because of what current treatment does. Bronchodilators and corticosteroids, the standard asthma drugs, only address symptoms, the university said. Blocking the sensor would go after the cause. [1]

"Our findings suggest that regular, inhaled therapy targeting these sensors may inhibit, halt or prevent this process," said Professor Chris Corrigan of King's College London, who co-led the study. [1] Co-lead Dr. Bethan Mansfield of Cardiff University said such drugs "could potentially be delivered directly to the lungs using inhalers similar to those already used." [1]

The researchers say drugs that target the CaSR are already in clinical trials for other conditions and could be repurposed. [1]

Keep the limits in view. The drug results so far come from lab studies on tissue and cells, not from people with asthma. No trial has yet tested a CaSR blocker as an asthma treatment. Everything here comes from the universities' own release; we have not seen independent comment on the paper.

Air pollution is the leading environmental threat to human health worldwide, the university said, and 99% of people breathe air dirtier than World Health Organization guidelines. [1]
