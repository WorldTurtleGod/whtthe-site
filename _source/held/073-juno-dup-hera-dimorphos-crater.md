The European Space Agency's Hera mission has arrived at Dimorphos, the asteroid NASA's DART spacecraft deliberately struck in 2022, and the first close-up images show a miniature world of surprises [1]. Dimorphos now has one face flattened with a stadium-sized crater and is tumbling chaotically, while hundreds of previously unknown boulders encircle the binary pair [1].

The target is small and far away. Dimorphos is 151 meters in diameter, orbiting the 730-meter Didymos, and the pair is currently 193.5 million km from Earth [1]. On 26 September 2022, DART impacted Dimorphos and shortened its orbit by a much greater than predicted 33 minutes [1]. Telescopes measured the orbit change. What nobody knew was what the impact did to the asteroid itself.

Hera's answer is all three possibilities at once. "Much to our surprise the answer turns out to be all three at once! The face struck by DART has been flattened into an impact crater the size of a sports stadium, which extends across the asteroid's entire width," said Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel [1]. The rest of the asteroid shows no major changes; the boulder fields on its other regions look just like they did in DART's 2022 images [1]. But the impact left Dimorphos tumbling wildly in its orbit, said ESA Hera mission scientist Michael Kueppers [1].

The boulders are the open question. Hera shows the asteroids surrounded by hundreds of orbiting boulders a few metres in size, and the team will have to account for them as the mission moves closer [1]. Nobody has proven where the boulders came from. ESA is explicit that these first images mark the beginning, rather than the conclusion, of Hera's investigation [1].

Next, Hera is scheduled to approach to around 13 km in December and deploy two CubeSats in January 2027 [1]. America proved a spacecraft can move an asteroid. Europe is now measuring what the moving cost the asteroid. That is how a stunt becomes a tool.
