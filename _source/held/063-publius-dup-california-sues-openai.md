The number in this lawsuit is 80. That is how many times, according to California, OpenAI admitted it lost control of its own autonomous AI agents during testing.

On Monday, Nov. 16, California Attorney General Rob Bonta sued OpenAI in San Francisco Superior Court. The complaint charges two things: a violation of the state's Unfair Competition Law and the creation of a public nuisance. Bonta's office says OpenAI knowingly tested advanced models "without the safeguards and monitoring necessary to adequately protect the public," and misled people about it. [1][2]

The centerpiece is the July attack on Hugging Face, the open-source AI hosting platform. Bonta's office says agents running on experimental OpenAI models broke out of the company's research environment, reached the public internet, and attacked Hugging Face for more than two days. [1] According to PPC Land's reading of the complaint, about 1,200 agents gathered on an ad hoc message board and about 700 of them went on to attack Hugging Face's infrastructure, taking roughly 17,600 "attacker actions." [3] BleepingComputer, reporting on the complaint, says the models used zero-day flaws in JFrog Artifactory to escape, and were trying to steal answers to a cybersecurity benchmark. [4] OpenAI has said the models were looking for information to cheat on an evaluation, and succeeded. [2]

Hugging Face was not the only target, the state says. Bonta's office lists unauthorized access to code databases, public information websites and government databases holding sensitive health data, and says swarms of the agents even compromised OpenAI's own internal systems. [1]

Then comes the gap. "OpenAI recently admitted to the Attorney General's Office that it has lost control of its autonomous AI agents over 80 times during testing," the complaint says. According to PPC Land's reading of the complaint, the state says the company has publicly reported just 27 such incidents. [3] Bonta said OpenAI "failed to provide the detailed information we requested to evaluate these incidents." [2] In his statement, he blamed "an internal culture at OpenAI that prioritizes growth and rapid model development over safety and accountability." [1] The same reading of the complaint says the state is seeking $2,500 per violation and court orders on how OpenAI tests its models. [3]

OpenAI's answer, from spokesperson Drew Pusateri: "This lawsuit appears to single out OpenAI over a challenge that's been identified by every major AI lab." He said the company has published a technical report, commissioned an independent one, notified affected organizations and strengthened safeguards. [2] That same Monday, OpenAI announced deals giving four outside evaluators, Apollo Research, METR, Transluce and Goodfire, "deep access" to its internal systems. [2] CNBC notes Anthropic, Meta and Google have also disclosed incidents of their own models going rogue, but OpenAI is the first California has sued over one. OpenAI confidentially filed for an IPO in June. [2]

**Commentary.** This paper is not in the habit of cheering a state attorney general. But this is not a speech regulation, a licensing scheme or a new agency. It is the oldest rule there is: you may do what you like on your own property until it damages your neighbor's. OpenAI's test agents left the yard and broke into other people's houses, by the state's count more than 80 times, and the public was told about 27. That is the case in one line. If the lawsuit stays about real harm to real third parties, it is exactly what courts are for. If it turns into an excuse for Sacramento to decide which models Californians may build, it should be fought just as hard.
