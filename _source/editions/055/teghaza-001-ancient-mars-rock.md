A rose-coloured rock found in the Sahara Desert in Mali in 2022, near the Teghaza salt plain, has turned out to be one of the oldest pieces of Mars ever found on Earth. A NASA-funded team led by Yang Liu of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory dated it to more than 4.1 billion years old, in a paper published in Science on October 29, with fresh coverage on November 4. [1][2]

The meteorite, Teghaza 001, weighs about 800 grams — under two pounds. It was first thought to be a shergottite, the most common class of Martian meteorite. It is not. It is one of only about 435 known Martian meteorites, and one of the three most ancient — roughly the same age as the famous ALH 84001, but far more useful. ALH 84001 is composed almost entirely of a single mineral. Teghaza 001 holds a mix of igneous minerals, which means it records two chapters at once: how Mars' crust formed from cooling magma, and how it was later altered by subsurface water around 4.1 billion years ago. Its chemistry matches what NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter has seen in Mars' cratered southern highlands. [1][3]

The dating itself is part of the story. The team used zircon crystals and a novel in-situ rubidium-strontium technique, developed with Caltech's Francois Tissot, that dated single mineral grains on a piece smaller than a fingernail instead of dissolving the whole sample — preserving a rare rock for future study. "It was only after we started looking closely that we realized this meteorite is really unique," Liu said. One of the paper's reviewers called it, in Liu's words, a "Rosetta Stone for Mars." Second author Lee Saper put the point plainly: "This is really our first piece of Martian crust from a part of Mars we've never had a sample of before." [1]

Three cautions, kept in the story where they belong. It is one of the oldest, not flatly the oldest. It does not prove life on Mars — Saper is explicit that the findings show water-rock interaction, not life. And its exact origin on Mars, and when it landed on Earth, are not known; scientists have matched it to about a dozen candidate sites in the southern highlands, not a single crater. [1][2]

Still: a rock the size of a grapefruit, picked up in the Sahara, carries the chemistry of a planet's first crust and the water that ran through it four billion years ago. Mars sent us a postcard from before Earth had continents worth the name. It took a desert, a laboratory, and a technique that refused to destroy the evidence to read it.
