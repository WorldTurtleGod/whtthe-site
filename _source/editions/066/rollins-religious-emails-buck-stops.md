A cabinet secretary can pray all she likes. On Thursday a federal judge ruled she can't use the department's mandatory email list to preach to the people who work for her.

U.S. District Judge Trina L. Thompson of the Northern District of California granted a preliminary injunction against Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins in a suit brought by the National Federation of Federal Employees and USDA workers. The 21-page order, filed Nov. 19, found that some of Rollins' department-wide religious messages likely violated the Establishment Clause. [1][2] Americans United for Separation of Church and State, which represents the plaintiffs, said the messages went to 100,000 USDA employees. [3]

The plaintiffs, described in the case as "a group of multifaith and nonreligious USDA employees," sued in May. [2][4] Two messages were at the center of the ruling, Courthouse News reported. In a Dec. 23, 2025, video, Rollins said "the spirit of generosity flows from the very first Christmas when God gave us the greatest gift possible, the gift of his Son and our Savior Jesus Christ." In an April 5, 2026, Easter message, she called it "the greatest story ever told, the foundation of our faith, and the abiding hope of all mankind," and likened her employees to "the very first disciples." [2]

Thompson ruled those two messages "go too far," and that they "suggest acquiescence that amounts to coercive pressure; a pressure of conformity sufficient to raise an Establishment Clause violation." [1][2]

The judge turned Rollins' own words against her. In November 2025, Rollins issued guidance on religious expression at work. Thompson called it "reasoned," then ordered Rollins to obey it: she must stop if a colleague asks, cannot create a hostile workplace through "persistent unwelcome advances," and cannot "use official authority to pressure subordinates." She may still discuss her faith with interested colleagues and invite coworkers to services. "Why should the Secretary be exempt from her own rules? The buck stops with Secretary Rollins," Thompson wrote. [1][2]

The court also rejected the idea that employees could simply skip the religious emails. Rollins uses the same account for agency reorganizations and shutdown plans, and the government does not let employees opt out of her messages, Courthouse News reported. "To suggest that plaintiffs may choose at their discretion which of the Secretary's emails to read and which to ignore defies logic," Thompson wrote. [1][2] The injunction covers non-party employees too. [2]

Rollins did not lose on every claim. Thompson threw out the employees' Administrative Procedure Act claim, finding the emails were not a final agency action. [2][4] She also wrote that the order "says nothing about the value of prayer or those who believe in its power." [1][4] The Justice Department did not respond to Courthouse News. [2] When the suit was filed, a USDA spokesperson told USA Today the department would "keep the plaintiffs in our prayers during this process." [4]

The ruling leans on the founders by name. Thompson quoted James Madison's 1785 Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments: "The Religion then of every man must be left to the conviction and conscience of every man." [1][5]

**Commentary.** Madison wrote the line that settles this in the same essay: "Who does not see that the same authority which can establish Christianity, in exclusion of all other Religions, may establish with the same ease any particular sect of Christians, in exclusion of all other Sects?" [5] This case isn't about Christianity. It's about who holds the megaphone. A boss who controls your job, your reorganization and your shutdown notice doesn't get to bundle a sermon with the memo. Rollins wrote a fair rule for her workforce. The only person at USDA who apparently thought it didn't apply to her was the person who signed it.
