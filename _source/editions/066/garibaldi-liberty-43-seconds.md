Some records take years of training and a laboratory. This one took 43.5 seconds, a canvas, and a flip.

On Thursday, November 19 — the 22nd annual Guinness World Records Day — performance painter David Garibaldi of Sacramento, California, set the Guinness World Records title for the fastest time to paint a landmark statue, painting the Statue of Liberty in 43.5 seconds on the Brooklyn waterfront, with the Brooklyn Bridge behind him and a crowd of delighted onlookers in front [1][2]. He swept streaks of green and blue paint into what looked like an abstract image, then flipped the canvas upside-down to reveal Lady Liberty's recognizable face [1]. It was his first Guinness World Records title [1].

Garibaldi is not a novelty act who wandered in off the street. He has over two decades of painting experience, first found widespread fame as a finalist on America's Got Talent in 2012, and is known for large-scale portraits painted in minutes while dancing to music — work he often auctions for charity [1]. His preparation for a sub-minute record was unglamorous and exact: paints within reach, the carpet positioned so he would not slip, and a clear image of the subject in his mind — and, by his account, a "flow state" despite the clock [1].

His own account of the attempt is the best copy in the story. "This was something that had never been done, which was – let's take the performance out of it, let's just go for speed," he said. "And I wanted to set this new standard for myself, and most importantly, for the Earth" [1]. And the pitch to everyone watching: "I hope this inspires a kid out there, or an adult, that thinks, look – I'm gonna go for this, and here's the worst part: while going for it, you will still become great in the process" [1]. On the feeling itself: "The feeling of breaking the record was a release of – I think – of any self doubt that I've had on this journey…" [1].

Guinness adjudicator Michael Empric, who was present for the attempt, supplied the official delight: "We really lucked out with the chance to work in such an iconic location, which only enhanced David's incredible skills as an artist," and, "Guinness World Records is all about trying to do something bigger than yourself. Guinness World Records Day is all about celebrating those iconic achievements. And to do it here in New York on the waterfront, and to have it be the Statue of Liberty is perfect" [1].

Garibaldi was one act in a full day of sanctioned absurdity — Guinness's own GWR Day roundup confirms his title and time, and adds, among others, Britain's Alastair Moffatt claiming the tightest parallel park driving a car on two wheels, with a total gap of just 6.53 metres [2]. Parade's coverage the same evening corroborates the 43.5 seconds, the Brooklyn crowd, and the upside-down reveal [3].

Forty-three and a half seconds. Most of us cannot find our keys in 43.5 seconds. A man from Sacramento painted the Statue of Liberty in that time, upside-down, backwards, in public — and the trick was twenty years of practice arranged so the paint was in reach and the carpet didn't slip. The record book, for once, is exactly right about what deserves a page.
