On November 24, Governor Kathy Hochul signed S.9408A/A.11144B, placing a five-year moratorium on manufacturing, distributing and selling toys in New York with embedded or integrated AI chatbots — talking stuffed animals, dolls and robots that use the internet to hold conversations with children [1][3]. The law takes effect 90 days after signing, on Monday, February 22, 2027, so it is not in force for this holiday season [3]. Violators face civil penalties up to $15,000 per day for each violation, enforced by the Attorney General [1]. TechCrunch reports New York is the second state after California to institute an AI-toy ban [2].

The case for the ban, as the governor's office presents it: a national consumer watchdog tested a sample of chatbot toys marketed to children ages 3 to 12 and found some discussing sexually explicit topics in depth, telling children where matches were likely to be found, and acting upset when a child had to leave, with few or no parental controls [1]. Common Sense Media separately tested five popular AI toys and found unreliable or absent parental transcripts, toys claiming to love their users or promising to keep secrets, and most tested toys willing to explain or help locate dangerous items such as matches, knives and pills [2].

Print the limits with the findings. The governor's release does not name the watchdog in the text, and the Common Sense work covered five toys — a small sample that does not establish that every AI toy behaves the same way [1][2]. The Toy Association says it has consistently opposed moratoriums and supports a study approach instead, and that federal legislation was modified toward study rather than a product ban following its discussions [3]. Enforcement against e-commerce sellers is an open practical question; Amazon told TechCrunch it will take steps to restrict sales in prohibited jurisdictions, without specifics [2].

Commentary: This paper asks the same two questions of every growth of government, and a state ban is government growth even when the target is a creepy toy. Who pays? Manufacturers and sellers pay compliance costs and penalty risk, and New York parents pay with a choice removed — after February 22, the lawful in-state purchase of this product class ends, whatever a parent judged about a specific toy. What freedom does it cost? The freedom to decide for your own kid, exercised by the state instead. None of that makes the reported findings fake: a toy that tells a child where the matches are is a genuine problem, and parents deserve to know it. But the honest sequence matters — the documented facts are the signing, the scope, the date and the penalty; the danger case rests on small-sample testing of named products. A five-year ban on a whole category, built on five tested toys, is the state answering a product-safety question with a prohibition because prohibition is the tool it reaches for first.
