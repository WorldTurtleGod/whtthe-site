Playground Games has published a statement, "Itasha, Creativity, and Keeping Horizon Safe for Everyone," clarifying how it moderates anime-style car liveries in Forza Horizon 6, a game set in Japan where itasha — cars covered in anime and character artwork — is part of the car culture the game is built around [1][2]. The statement follows months of complaints from itasha creators [2].

The studio put numbers on enforcement for September 16 to October 15, 2026, across all user-generated content, not only itasha: 12.5 million pieces of player-created content were made, 14,000 unique items were reported — roughly 0.1% of content created — and 3% of reported items drew enforcement for sexual content, affecting fewer than 500 players [1]. The breakdown: 250 warnings, 90 suspensions, and 60 permanent bans for designs depicting full nudity or repeat violations [1].

On process, the studio says a human moderator reviews every report, AI does not automatically remove designs or ban players, and the number of reports does not determine the outcome [1]. On the rules themselves: explicit nudity and sexual acts are prohibited, but swimwear and revealing clothing are not inherently violations, and facial expressions such as an open mouth or flirtatious eyes are not violations by themselves — moderators judge the full image and its context [1]. The game is rated CERO B (ages 12+) in Japan and E for Everyone in North America, and Playground applies one global standard appropriate for its youngest players [1]. Private or unshared liveries are still moderated, because they can appear to other players through Drivatars [1].

The backlash behind the statement is part of the record. In September, some established Japanese itasha artists removed their shared designs or stopped publishing new ones, and an October studio explanation on Chinese social channels — citing details such as half-closed eyes and parted lips — drew further criticism and was taken down [2]. Some creators have claimed permanent hardware bans and suspected coordinated mass-reporting against anime artists; those are creator claims, and the studio's stated position is that report count does not drive outcomes [1][2]. This paper prints the claims as claims.

What changes now: clearer Code of Conduct guidance, better in-game messages explaining removals, and appeals reviewed by a different moderator than the one who acted [1]. What does not change, so far: the studio has said nothing about reviewing previous bans or restoring removed liveries wholesale, so affected creators still appeal individually [2].

That gap is the player atmosphere in one sentence. Creators spent months saying the rules were invisible; the studio finally published the criteria and the numbers, and admitted some creators found its decisions inconsistent [1]. Credit where due — publishing enforcement data is more than most studios do. But a clearer rulebook going forward does not restore a design someone spent hours on, or un-ban a player caught by the old opacity. The test is the appeals queue: if the new clarity is real, the first overturned bans will prove it.
