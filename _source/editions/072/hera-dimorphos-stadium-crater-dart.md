In September 2022, NASA slammed a spacecraft into a small asteroid on purpose to see whether people could knock a space rock off course. It worked. This week we finally saw what the punch did to the rock.

On Wednesday, November 25, the European Space Agency released the first close-up images of Dimorphos from its Hera spacecraft. [1][2] Dimorphos is a moonlet 151 meters across that orbits a larger asteroid, Didymos, which is 730 meters across. The pair is currently 193.5 million kilometers from Earth and poses no threat to us. [1][4] NASA's DART spacecraft hit Dimorphos on September 26, 2022, at about 22,000 km/h, or 14,000 mph, and shortened its orbit around Didymos by 33 minutes. That was far more than predicted. [1][3][4]

**What Hera found.** "Scientists debated whether Dimorphos had sustained an impact crater, changed its shape or stayed the same," said Hera principal investigator Patrick Michel of France's CNRS and the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur. "Much to our surprise the answer turns out to be all three at once! The face struck by DART has been flattened into an impact crater the size of a sports stadium, which extends across the asteroid's entire width." [1]

The key image was taken November 7 from 29 kilometers away, at 2.7 meters per pixel. It shows odd structures inside the crater, including what ESA calls "an apparent cigar-shaped structure at its centre." [2][3] The rest of Dimorphos looks the way it did in DART's last pictures from 2022, and Didymos looks unchanged. [1][2]

The rock is also no longer behaving. "Before the Dart impact, Dimorphos was more like our own Moon - so it was always showing the same face to Didymos," Hera team planetary scientist Sabina Raducan told the BBC. "Now it's very, very chaotic - it's tumbling. Imagine a barrel that you throw down the stairs." [4] "It's complete chaos," Hera mission manager Ian Carnelli told Euronews. [5]

Then there's the gravel. Hera spotted hundreds of boulders orbiting the pair, and the mission will have to steer around them as it moves closer. [1][4] The sources disagree on their size. ESA's release says "just a few metres in size." [1] The BBC says they range "from tens of centimetres to metre-size rocks." [4] Euronews says "up to a metre." [5]

**What's next.** In December, Hera is scheduled to close to about 13 kilometers. In January 2027 it plans to release two shoebox-sized CubeSats, Juventas and Milani, which will eventually try to land on the asteroid. [1][3] The main goal is to weigh Dimorphos, because the mass tells scientists how hard the shove really was. Hera scientist Simon Green told the BBC the data should let planners say, in his words, "we need this mass of spacecraft, travelling at this speed, this early, in order to deflect it enough so that it misses the Earth." [4]

This was a joint effort. Germany paid about 130 million euros, roughly 37% of the mission, and Bremen-based OHB built the spacecraft, according to Germany's space agency DLR. [3] Japan's JAXA supplied the TIRI thermal camera on board. [1][5] Apophis, a roughly 450-meter asteroid, will pass about 32,000 kilometers from Earth on April 13, 2029. ESA and JAXA's Ramses mission and NASA's OSIRIS-APEX plan to meet it. [1][4]

**Commentary.** The Constitution's preamble names "the common defence" among the reasons the federal government exists at all. [6] Few things fit that phrase better than a rock that could flatten a city. Most federal spending can't be explained in a sentence. This can: we hit an asteroid, we moved it, and now we know what hitting it does. It's also the right kind of foreign partnership. Nobody is choosing sides in anybody's war. Europe, Japan and the United States split the cost of a job that protects every one of them.
