The Federal Communications Commission has authorized SpaceX to deploy 1 million satellites in space for data centers powering artificial intelligence [1]. The constellation is called Starmind, made of 1 million "orbital compute modules" that would process massive amounts of data to be used back on Earth [1][2].

The scale deserves a number next to it. There are currently around 16,700 operational satellites in space according to Orbital Radar, with the European Space Agency putting functioning satellites at 15,800 [1]. Washington just licensed roughly sixty times the current working satellite population, for one company's AI compute plan.

The license is a schedule, not a launch. SpaceX must deploy at least one authorized space station by November 2033, 10% of the constellation — 100,000 satellites — by November 2035, and 50% by November 2038 [1]. Authorization is not deployment, and SpaceX itself cautioned in a regulatory filing ahead of its June IPO that orbital data centers rely on "unproven" technologies and "may not achieve commercial viability" [1].

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr sold it as a frontier: "This action opens a bold new frontier for the computing infrastructure needed to power America's leadership and growth" [1]. Elon Musk welcomed the decision on X, saying "this will be a great asset to America and the world" [1].

The skeptics are named, not anonymous. Astrophysicist Adam Becker called the million-satellite prospect "a fantasy" and said, "It's not going to happen" [2]. The American Astronomical Society said it was "dismayed" by the decision, warning the satellites would be visible as extremely bright objects and threaten astronomy research [1][2]. The FCC maintained the constellation operates consistently with its orbital debris rules and that deployment "serves the public interest" [2].

The take: a federal license for a million machines in low Earth orbit is government power at its most literal — Washington handing out the sky. Who pays if the debris math is wrong? Every weather satellite, every telescope, every American who looks up. What freedom does it cost? The freedom of the night sky itself, which nobody voted to give away. America leading in AI is a fine goal. It does not require pretending a license is a constellation, or that the public interest was proven rather than asserted.
