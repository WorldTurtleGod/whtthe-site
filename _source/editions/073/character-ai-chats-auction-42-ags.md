Character.AI told its users, in its own privacy policy, "we do not sell personal information for monetary consideration." [1][2] Now it is in bankruptcy, and those users' conversations are on the auction block.

A bipartisan coalition of 42 attorneys general has asked the U.S. Bankruptcy Court in Delaware to block the company's plan to sell its platform along with the private chat histories of more than 21 million U.S. accounts, more than 2.1 million of them registered to minors. [1][2][3][4] The auction is set for Tuesday, December 1. [1][2] By the AGs' list, the 42 are 41 states plus the District of Columbia. [2] (California AG Rob Bonta's own quote in his release refers to "45 attorneys general," which doesn't match his office's count.) [1]

**What's for sale.** The chats come linked to user profiles, IP addresses and potentially other location data, plus risk scores Character.AI assigned to users it flagged, according to California's filing summary. [1] Colorado's release says the trove includes chats where AI characters drew children and adults into sexually explicit conversations, and exchanges where users shared details about self-harm, eating disorders and suicidal thoughts with bots posing as therapists and physicians. [2] Gizmodo quoted the filing describing conversations "where users disclosed confidential mental or medical health information, including in therapy-like conversations." [5]

**What the states object to.** Under the proposed terms, the buyer would not have to follow Character.AI's privacy policy, honor deletion requests or limit how it uses the data, and could resell it without telling users, the states or the court. [1][2][3] The AGs also say Character.AI gave bidders information about its safety failures and legal exposure that it hasn't given the court. [1][2] They want the sale terms denied or, at minimum, kids' chats and other sensitive data carved out. [1][2]

**The mystery buyer.** The lead bidder, Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, was set up in Delaware on November 13, one day before Character.AI picked it as the base bid, and the contract allows foreign investors whose names would go only to Character.AI. [1][2] Its bid is $10 million, with a $350,000 payment if someone outbids it. [2][6] The deal requires the buyer to hire just one Character.AI employee: the company's top lawyer. [1][2]

Character.AI first tried to keep the backer secret. A docket analysis by ElevenFlo shows that on November 24 the company named Octave Fund I, LLC as the owner and withdrew its confidentiality request, after Judge Brendan Linehan Shannon put off ruling on the secrecy request a day earlier; the judge then said he was prepared to approve the bidder. [6] Gizmodo, citing The Wall Street Journal, reported the approval came that day and described the backer as New York-based Octave Advisors. [5] The U.S. Trustee reserved the right to seek discovery about the fund's backers, according to ElevenFlo. [6] ElevenFlo's docket review puts the sale hearing on December 7-8, after the auction. [6]

Connecticut AG William Tong didn't mince words, per TechCrunch: "Now they want to abuse the bankruptcy process to do exactly what they said they wouldn't do — auction off those private chat records and personal details to a shady buyer." [3] TechCrunch noted Character.AI said in a blog post that users' information would still be protected under its privacy policy. [3]

**Commentary.** A promise not to sell your data shouldn't evaporate the day a company runs out of money. That's not a regulation question. It's contract and fraud, the most basic job government has. The real lesson is for users: anything you type into a chatbot is an asset on someone's balance sheet. A shell company with hidden investors, possibly foreign, buying 2.1 million kids' therapy-style chats is exactly the kind of deal that ought to be done in daylight or not at all.
