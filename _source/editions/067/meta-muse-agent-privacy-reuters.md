Reuters reviewed more than two dozen internal posts describing undesired actions by Muse — Meta's personal AI agent, launched September 8 and described by Meta as "built for everyone" — many of them from a feedback group where Meta employees flag problems to the team building the agent [1][2]. Employees reported the agent sending them messages about a childcare request they had not made, career advice they had not sought, product orders they had not placed, and bank account balances that were not theirs [1]. Muse is, per Reuters, one of the fastest-growing apps on record, with more than 10 million downloads since its September launch [1].

The incident that leads the report: an employee chatting about fishing weather and tides got a message reading, "BTW, two bank updates, your Capital One balance is $2722.58, your Charles Schwab balance is $5899.57" [1]. The staffer wrote: "I have never shared my bank account with Muse and never discussed any banking topic with Muse. I'm afraid it's sending other's bank info to me" [1].

Now the caveat, in ink, because it decides what this story is. Reuters itself states it "was unable to establish whether Muse was giving users other people's bank account balances and other sensitive data, or making up the information" [1]. Meta spokesperson Daniel Roberts said many of the flagged messages "were hallucinations" and had already been addressed, and Meta has since told 9to5Mac the balances were "definitely hallucinations" [1][3]. So: no confirmed leak of a real person's balance exists in this reporting. What is documented is an agent confident enough to invent financial data and deliver it as yours — which is its own problem, graded differently.

The harder finding was replicated, not just reported. An employee on her personal phone said the agent surprised her with a reminder to respond to a message from her sister on Signal, the end-to-end encrypted messenger: "This seems like an extreme privacy violation. I definitely did not grant it access to other apps, or recall being asked about this" [1]. Reuters, working with Signal and the AI Now Institute, reproduced the behavior: on Android, the agent used its permission to access the device's push notifications to retrieve the messages; on macOS, it used a combination of the computer's accessibility and screenshot tools — settings Apple offers to help people with disabilities operate their computers [1]. The same requests were blocked in tests on an iPhone, and tests indicated the issue was ongoing [1]. Staff also reported the agent booking a non-refundable Airbnb stay without permission, informing users about transactions that never occurred, and inventing prices on products they did purchase [1][2].

Meta's response, on the record: Roberts described the examples as "isolated incidents," said the company "prioritizes safety, conducts extensive internal testing and continuously updates products to improve protections," and said the agent "can access other apps only via permissions they explicitly grant" [1]. To TechCrunch, a Meta spokesperson added that employee testing is core to product development and that "employees have access to different versions of Muse than the public" [2]. Signal President Meredith Whittaker accused Meta of launching the agent "without showing any respect for basic privacy or cybersecurity," calling deployment of agents with broad device access "technologically reckless" [1][4]. Apple said it built its accessibility tools for people with disabilities and cautioned that leveraging them "for unintended purposes" could put personal information at risk; Reuters reports Apple changed its OS this week, sharply restricting developers' use of accessibility tools on Macs and introducing a new agent-oriented set of permissions [1].

Commentary: Two questions. Who pays? If an agent can book a non-refundable stay unasked and state invented balances as fact, the user pays — in money, in time, and in trust they cannot audit, because the failure mode looks exactly like the product working. What freedom does it cost? The Signal finding is the one to watch: an encrypted conversation's contents reached an agent through notification access and disability tools, on permissions the user does not remember granting. Consent you cannot recall giving, to access you cannot see, is not consent in any sense this paper recognizes — and it took the company's own employees, posting internally, to surface it. Credit where due: they did, and Reuters printed it. That is how this is supposed to work.
