The Scripps Institution of Oceanography at UC San Diego announced in a Thursday, November 19 Facebook post that Portuguese man o' war had been found stranded in San Diego County — near the tidepools at Cabrillo National Monument, and at Del Mar and La Jolla beaches — an appearance Scripps calls very rare for Southern California [1][2]. Scripps' explanation: "warmer ocean waters associated with El Niño may be creating conditions for these unusual visitors to show up along our coastline" [1].

What washed up is stranger than it looks. The Portuguese man o' war, Physalia, is not a true jellyfish and not even a single animal: per NOAA, "A siphonophore is unusual in that it is comprised of a colony of specialized, genetically identical individuals called zooids — clones — with various forms and functions, all working together as one" [1]. Its balloon-like float may be blue, violet or pink and "rises up to six inches above the waterline," resembling an 18th-century Portuguese warship under full sail [1]. Below the sail: tentacles averaging 30 feet and extending as much as 100 feet, carrying stinging nematocysts that can paralyze and kill small fish and crustaceans [1]. They drift with winds and currents, normally in tropical and subtropical seas, and can gather in groups — legions — of 1,000 or more [2].

Linsey Sala, manager of the Pelagic Invertebrate Collection at Scripps Oceanography: "This is an exciting occurrence for this region and we are grateful to the curious and careful naturalists who reported them" [1][2]. And the warning, from Scripps: "Please be aware, this passive drifter does have specialized stinging cells called nematocysts and can deliver a painful sting" [1]. The National Ocean Service: "While the man o' war's sting is rarely deadly to people, it packs a painful punch and causes welts on exposed skin" [2]. Sala again: "Knowledge is power, and knowing that these gorgeous animals may periodically be here allows us to keep an eye out and aim to avoid touching them while on beach walks or swims. They are quite common in a number of other places worldwide like Hawaii and Australia" [1]. Scripps invites photos and sightings reports through its El Niño Fish Records Form [1]. If stung, the reported first aid is to rinse with seawater, carefully remove visible tentacles, and immerse the skin in hot water until the pain subsides [2].

One identification note for beach walkers: the man o' war's blue-to-purple hue resembles Velella velella, the by-the-wind sailors that turn up in California in winter and spring — but Physalia carries the gas-filled float and the long trailing tentacles, and it is the one that stings [1].

A colony of clones sailing under a pink balloon, a hundred feet of sting trailing behind it, blown onto beaches where it almost never belongs by a warming patch of ocean. It is gorgeous, it is genuinely dangerous, and the correct relationship with it is admiration from a distance — which, the naturalists of San Diego have just demonstrated, is a relationship Californians can manage.
