Peter Fluck, the cartoonist and puppeteer who co-created Spitting Image, the British TV show that mocked the powerful with latex puppets, has died at 85. [1][2]

His family said he died early Saturday, Nov. 21, at home in Cornwall after a recent illness, the BBC reported. [1][2] "The spirit that Spitting Image brought to the country was epoch-defining," the family statement said. "He was a private man who eschewed fame and attention." [1][2]

**The show.** Spitting Image ran on ITV from 1984 to 1996, 18 series in all. It featured puppet caricatures of politicians, royals and celebrities. [1][2] Margaret Thatcher was played as an aggressive "iron lady," and Liberal leader David Steel as the weak sidekick of David Owen, the Guardian reported. [2] Her successor John Major became, in the BBC's words, "a dull, grey puppet with a penchant for peas." [1] The show won a BAFTA TV award and two Emmys, according to the BBC. [1] **On audience size the outlets disagree.** The BBC says the show drew 15 million viewers at its peak, and the Guardian says about 18 million. [1][2]

Fluck created it with Roger Law, a friend he met at Cambridge School of Art. In 1976 the two set up a caricature partnership called "Luck and Flaw." [1] Fluck told the Guardian in 2014 that they turned to puppets when the illustrators were pushed out of Fleet Street: "when Murdoch started buying up Fleet Street, the illustrators and caricaturists were booted out." [2] The puppets were life-size, he said, and "clothes are far cheaper from Oxfam than a wardrobe department." [2]

"You could say we took the traditional newspaper editorial cartoon and put it on the screen," Fluck told ArtCornwall in 2006, as quoted by the BBC. He said the show aired "at the difficult time of 10 o'clock on a Sunday night when all politicians should have been in bed." [1]

**The powerful wanted in.** "We did get quite a lot of approaches from politicians for their puppet to be made," Fluck said in a 2011 interview quoted by the BBC, "because if you didn't appear as a puppet on Spitting Image you hadn't made it as a politician." [1] The royals were cut from the first episode "as a matter of courtesy" to Prince Philip, who was opening the network's new studios, the Guardian reported, citing the Times. After that they appeared all the time. [2] "A wonderful thing was the small amount of really vicious hate mail that would come in on Monday morning," Fluck said in 2014. "It would have been rather regrettable if we hadn't." [2]

ITV ended the show as ratings fell. [1] In a statement after his death, ITV said Fluck's "creative contribution to TV satire is simply unrivalled." [1][2] After the show, he moved to Cornwall and worked in ceramics, printmaking and mobiles. [1] Law donated the Spitting Image archive, including scripts, puppet molds and drawings, to Cambridge University in 2018. [1]

**Commentary.** America's founders had this same fight early. Seven years after the First Amendment, Congress passed the Sedition Act of 1798, which made "any false, scandalous and malicious writing" against the government a crime. The only journalists prosecuted under it edited opposition newspapers, and the backlash helped defeat the Federalists in 1800, the National Archives notes. [3] Fluck's whole career rested on the opposite idea: people in power deserve less respect, not more, and a free country laughs at them every Sunday night. Britain gave him that freedom, and he used it on every prime minister and the monarchy. That was worth more than any statue.
