Federal workers looking at a possible Dec. 12 shutdown have one protection they did not have last fall: agencies have agreed not to use a lapse in funding to start layoffs. That promise has an expiration date, and Congress's likely next move could push the next deadline past it.

**The deal.** During the 43-day shutdown last fall, agencies sent about 4,200 reduction-in-force (RIF) notices to employees at Commerce, Education, Health and Human Services, Homeland Security, Housing and Urban Development, Treasury and the EPA, Government Executive reported. [3] The unions sued as AFGE v. OMB in federal court in San Francisco, and the case was settled Sept. 23. [1][3] In the agreement, the defendant agencies promised to remove from their "operative contingency plans" any language that "provides for initiation of RIFs during a lapse in appropriations" or that lets employees do RIF paperwork as excepted work, unless a statute expressly allows it. [1]

On Oct. 23, OMB Director Russell Vought and OPM Director Scott Kupor signed memo M-27-01. It tells agency heads that the September 2025 OMB email and OPM guidance authorizing shutdown RIFs "have since been rescinded in full and are no longer operative." [2]

**The catch is in the settlement text.** The plan provision "shall continue to apply only to contingency plans operative through December 31, 2026." [1] Even before then, agencies can put RIF language back in, as long as they notify the unions and publish the change 30 days before it takes effect. [1] The case is on hold and is set to be dismissed with prejudice at the earlier of Dec. 31 or the enactment of full-year fiscal 2027 funding for every agency defendant. The unions can ask to reopen it only if an agency gives notice of a plan change before then. [1] The settlement also states that no one admits any facts or law. [1]

**Why the date matters now.** Funding runs out after Dec. 11. Reuters reported Nov. 20 that both parties "aim to find common ground on a stopgap measure to fund government operations into early next year," after Democrats take both chambers on Jan. 3. [4] A shutdown starting Dec. 12 would begin under plans with the RIF language removed, though one that ran past New Year's would outlast the promise. A lapse at the end of a stopgap that runs into January or February would come after the settlement's plan obligation has expired. At that point, what protects workers is whatever OPM and OMB are saying then.

Reuters also reported the obstacles. Democrats are split over whether to insist on limiting money for the Iran war. "We don't want to fund the war, we don't want to shut down, how do you solve that," Sen. Peter Welch, D-Vt., said. "That's the Gordian knot." [4] Senate Republicans dropped a party-line reconciliation bill for President Trump's $350 billion defense request for now in favor of bipartisan talks, but say reconciliation "remains on the table," Reuters reported. [4] Senate Democrats also face a Dec. 1 leadership vote between Chuck Schumer and Chris Van Hollen, which Republicans say is slowing talks. [4]

**What to check.** Back-pay law is covered in this paper's earlier shutdown reporting. On layoffs, ask your agency for its current lapse contingency plan and look for any RIF language. If the plan changes, the settlement requires public notice 30 days before the change takes effect. [1]

**Commentary.** This protection exists because unions sued and the government settled, not because Congress passed a law. It lasts as long as the settlement says, and the settlement says Dec. 31. When this administration used last fall's shutdown as a layoff tool, it took a court injunction and then a spending-bill rider to stop it. [3] If Congress wants federal workers kept out of its budget fights, it should put a ban on shutdown RIFs into the U.S. Code in the same stopgap it passes. A protection that exists only in a court settlement will not last past the settlement.
