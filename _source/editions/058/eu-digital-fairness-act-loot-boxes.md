For years the loot box has been the slot machine nobody was allowed to call a slot machine. On Wednesday, the European Commission came close to calling it one.

The Commission formally proposed its Digital Fairness Act on Nov. 11, after a draft leaked earlier in the week. [1][2][4] Commissioner Michael McGrath called it "the most ambitious reform of EU consumer protection rules in a generation." [3] Most of the package is about shopping in general: fake "almost sold out" warnings, hidden fees, subscriptions that are hard to cancel. [3] But four articles land squarely on games. [1][2]

Here is what they would do, according to the proposal as reported by Inven Global and heise online:

- **Loot boxes off-limits to minors.** Article 15 bans selling paid loot boxes to players under 18 outright. It covers boxes bought with real money or with purchased in-game currency, and heise reports that parents cannot override it. [1][2]
- **Odds on the label, and an off switch for everyone.** Any game that sells loot boxes would have to list what's inside and the chance of getting each item. All players, adults included, must get "effective tools" to turn paid loot boxes off. [1][2]
- **No pay-to-win for kids.** Article 16 extends the ban to "pay-to-progress and pay-to-win mechanisms" for minors, meaning purchases that are needed, or presented as needed, to get ahead. [1][2]
- **Gems get a price tag.** Article 14 says anything bought with a paid virtual currency must show its real-money value, or an estimate. For minors, paid currencies would be "disabled by default" and switched on only with a parent's approval. [1][2]
- **Turn off the nagging.** Article 17 would let players disable features built to encourage "compulsive or excessive use," such as rewards for logging in at set times and penalties for missing them. [1]

The draft explains why it treats loot boxes, wheels of fortune and card packs differently from a normal purchase: they "share the speculative features of gambling." [2]

The law would not stop at Europe's borders. It applies to any company selling to consumers in the EU, wherever it is based, so a studio in the U.S. or Asia would have to adapt its games for the European market. [2] A companion reform would let the Commission investigate big cross-border cases itself and fine companies up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover. [1][3]

The industry is not pleased. Germany's game industry association argues that treating every game rated under 18 as a children's game would make the long-standing USK and PEGI age ratings meaningless. "The age ratings from USK and PEGI, which have been successful for decades, would be rendered meaningless," managing director Felix Falk said, according to heise. [2] Money is the reason for the alarm: the association says in-game and in-app purchases brought in about €4.1 billion in Germany in 2025, against roughly €800 million from game sales. [2] Heise also points out a practical puzzle. In a mode like EA Sports FC's Ultimate Team, progress largely depends on packs, so an adult who "turns them off" may simply fall behind. [2]

None of this is law yet. The proposal now goes to the European Parliament and the Council of the EU, which must agree on a final text, and the rules would apply 12 months after the regulation takes effect. [1][2]

**Commentary.** An American reader can admire the disclosure rules and still wince at the reach. Printing the odds and the real price is the kind of honesty any free market runs better on. A ban that parents are not allowed to override is a different animal. It puts a regulator's judgment ahead of the family's, and on games sold by companies that never set foot in Brussels. The founders built a republic on the idea that decisions belong as close to the people as they can sit. Few decisions sit closer than what a kid can buy on the family console.
