Washington owns a great deal of the American West, and on Tuesday the Interior Department said it would like to own less of it.

The department's Fiscal Years 2026–2030 Strategic Plan, released Nov. 10, says Interior "will work to decrease the size of the federal estate by offering to states, localities and Tribes the opportunity to purchase federal lands." [1] The sales, the plan says, would let the department "focus its resources on primary federal responsibilities and nationally significant federal lands." A portion of the proceeds would go back to the buyers for conservation elsewhere in their state, locality or tribal land, "while at the same time decreasing the federal taxpayer's financial responsibility to manage federal lands." [1]

The plan pairs that with land exchanges, rights-of-way deals and "direct transfer of lands in support of reliable energy production and transport," and it says Interior will shift conservation spending toward easements on private land so "that the federal estate does not continue to grow." [1]

What the plan does not contain is a map. It names no parcels, no acreage totals, no prices and no schedule. [2][3] The Independent, carrying the Associated Press report, notes that a strategy document of this kind does not bind future administrations. [4]

The stakes are not small. Interior manages more than 750,000 square miles, an area larger than California and Texas combined. [3][4] The federal government owns about 80% of Nevada, 64% of Utah and 62% of Idaho, according to the Deseret News. [3]

Interior Secretary Doug Burgum framed the plan around access and energy. "We're in the business of access. We're trying to get more access to more people with what we're doing," he told the Deseret News in Utah on Wednesday. [3]

The pushback came fast, and from both parties. Rep. Ryan Zinke of Montana, a Republican who ran Interior in President Trump's first term, "remains firmly opposed to the sale or transfer of public lands," his office said, and he plans to vote against any such measure before he leaves Congress at the end of this term. [4] Sen. Martin Heinrich of New Mexico, a Democrat, said: "These lands belong to the American people, not private interests looking to cash in. Public lands belong in public hands." [3][4] Sen. Catherine Cortez Masto of Nevada said she is "prepared to fight" and is "concerned by the lack of details." [3]

Kate Groetzinger of the Center for Western Priorities, a conservation group, said the plan "goes well beyond" the Heritage Foundation's Project 2025 "by recommending public land sell-offs." [3] The last serious push in this direction, including Utah Sen. Mike Lee's proposal to transfer more than 2 million acres to states and others, collapsed last year amid opposition from both parties. [4]

**Commentary.** Strip away the slogans and this is a fight the founders saw coming. Article IV of the Constitution gives the power "to dispose of and make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States" to Congress, not to a department. [5] Writing in Federalist No. 43, James Madison called it "a power of very great importance," and noted that the proviso attached to it was needed because of "jealousies and questions concerning the Western territory." [6] Nearly two and a half centuries later, the jealousies are still about the West.

There is a respectable limited-government case for a smaller federal estate. A national government that owns four-fifths of a state is a strange landlord for a federal republic, and returning land to states and localities moves decisions closer to the people who live beside it. But there is an equally old principle on the other side. Property held in common for the whole country should change hands by the deliberate act of the people's representatives, in the open, with parcels named and prices known. WRE News notes that federal statutes limit how public lands can be disposed of, that some transactions could require congressional action, and that the plan "does not independently override those laws." [2] A plan with no parcels, no acreage and no vote is a statement of intent. Whether it becomes anything more is a question for Congress, which is where Madison left it.
