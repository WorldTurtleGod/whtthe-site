Karl wakes up, and the first face he sees is usually Luna's. She is his grey tabby. On some days, for the rest of the day, Luna's almond-shaped eye sockets, wide pupils and stripes show up on the human faces around him, in person and online. [1]

It runs the other way too. If a person is the first face Karl sees, that person's pupils and eye shape can turn up on other people, and on the cat. [1][2]

Karl, 28, lives in the northeastern United States. His case is described in a short report in the journal Cortex by Brad Duchaine of Dartmouth College, Dartmouth PhD student Antônio Mello and Jennifer Nosker, a neuropsychologist at Brigham and Women's Hospital. [1][3] Two years ago, surgeons removed part of his right temporal lobe to treat an arteriovenous malformation, a tangle of blood vessels that can bleed into the brain. The right temporal lobe plays a key role in recognizing faces. Afterward Karl developed palinopsia, in which something seen earlier lingers or reappears, and in his case it distorts faces, a condition called prosopometamorphopsia, or PMO. [1][2]

The episodes often last several hours and sometimes all day, the report says. The borrowed features don't look pasted on. They blend into the face and change naturally as the angle or distance shifts. [3] And they are not generic. Karl recognizes them as belonging to particular people he knows, or to Luna, which tells the researchers the parts are being pulled from long-term memory. [3]

"Our brains break faces into components and match them against features stored in memory," Duchaine told Gizmodo. "Karl's percepts are what happens when that matching goes wrong and a stored feature gets stuck in the face perception system." [2]

The case has a famous ancestor, and it appeared in the same journal. In 1986 Cortex reported on a woman, nine months after a stroke, who looked at a poodle on a bus passenger's lap. When she looked away, every passenger on the bus seemed to have the poodle's face. It wore off half an hour later. [3] The new report is titled "Forty years after poodle-face palinopsia." [3] The difference is that the poodle took over whole faces, while Karl's brain swaps in parts. [1][3]

There's a modern twist in how the researchers showed it. Instead of relying on rough sketches, they used ChatGPT 5.5 to turn drawings and Karl's descriptions into photorealistic images. Over several months they refined those images with Karl's feedback, and he made some edits himself with another AI tool, until he judged they matched what he saw. [3] Duchaine says his lab has heard from more than 250 people with face distortions, some of whom were told they had a psychiatric problem when the trouble was in their vision. [1]

Most of us will never see a friend wearing our cat's eyes. But Karl shows the rest of us something about how we see. A face is built from parts, quickly and from memory, every time we look.
