Nvidia reported earnings on Tuesday, Nov. 17, for the first time since co-founder Jensen Huang quit as CEO last week. The quarter covers the months before he left, and the numbers did not slow down. [4]

Revenue for the quarter ended Oct. 25 was $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year earlier and 20% from the previous quarter. Data center revenue was $107.6 billion, up 110%. GAAP net income was $65.3 billion. [1] CNBC reported that analysts surveyed by LSEG had expected $109.1 billion in revenue and $2.48 in adjusted earnings per share. Nvidia reported $2.68. [2] Nvidia guided fourth-quarter revenue to $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, and said that outlook assumes no data center compute revenue from China. [1]

Interim CEO Colette Kress, who is also the company's CFO, said: "Our momentum is real." On the call, she raised the forecast for the fiscal year ending January 2028 to about 75% revenue growth, up from 70%. [1][3] Board chair Harvey Jones said "the board does not intend to reinstate" Huang, according to the BBC, and said the search for a permanent CEO would include internal and external candidates. [4] The BBC also reported that Kress said Nvidia is no longer shipping Hopper data center chips to China, and did not say why. [4] CNBC reported the stock rose more than 3% in after-hours trading. [2]

**The rule that landed the day before.** On Monday, Nov. 16, the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security issued an interim final rule on remote access to advanced AI chips. BIS said in its release that the rule was "written by and at the direction of the Department of Treasury and the National Security Council." [5] According to Manufacturing Dive's summary, data centers outside the restricted countries now need a license if they supply significant computing power to companies headquartered in China, Russia, Iran, Macau or other Country Group D:5 nations. The rule also requires licenses to export advanced chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. Companies headquartered in the United States are exempt if they meet reporting conditions. [6] Kress mentioned the new controls on the call: "with demand far exceeding supply, we expect our performance to remain strong." [3]

**The President's portfolio.** CNBC reported on Nov. 16 that its analysis of President Trump's financial disclosures found his investment accounts bought or sold Nvidia shares at least 103 times since he returned to the White House, in transactions worth a combined $27 million to $97.5 million. One sale, worth $500,001 to $1 million, came on May 18. According to CNBC, citing The New York Times, that was five days after Secretary of State Marco Rubio briefed Trump on Huang's relationship with a suspected Chinese spy. Trump denied being briefed. The White House told CNBC that independent advisors manage the portfolio without his input, and CNBC said the disclosures show no evidence Trump directed the trades. [7]

**Commentary.** Three things are true at once. Nvidia builds the best AI chips in the world and its customers can't get enough of them. The executive branch, by an interim rule written at the direction of the Treasury and the NSC, now decides by license who in Thailand, Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia and Brazil may buy them. And the man at the top of that executive branch has accounts that trade the stock. Thailand is a longtime U.S. ally, [8] and nobody voted on making its companies ask Washington's permission. Congress should write export rules in law, in public, and it should finally ban officials at the top, starting with the President, from owning or trading individual stocks while they set policy for the companies involved. "Independent advisors" is not a firewall the founders would recognize.
