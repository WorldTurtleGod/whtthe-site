No, the Senate did not vote at 1 a.m. If you read overnight that the permitting bill had cleared its last gate, you read a schedule guess, not a result. Here is what the Senate's own floor schedule actually says.

The Senate stands adjourned until 10:00 a.m. on Wednesday, November 18, and resumes consideration of the Capito substitute amendment #6883 to H.R. 2347, post-cloture [1]. At approximately 11:45 a.m. ET, if no further agreement is reached, all post-cloture time is considered expired, and senators should expect up to three roll call votes, at about 11:30 a.m. onward: first, a motion to waive budget points of order against the substitute, if one is raised; second, adoption of the substitute itself; third, cloture on H.R. 2347 — the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act — as amended, which needs 60 votes [1][2].

Keep the ladder straight, because this week has punished everyone who didn't. November 9: cloture on the motion to proceed, 73–25. November 16: cloture on the substitute, 70–27, roll call 264 [2][3]. Today's third vote, if it happens, is the last 60-vote gate. None of today's votes passes the bill. Leaders have not announced a time for a final passage vote, and because H.R. 2347 is a House bill, the House would still have to approve whatever the Senate passes [2]. Reuters' explainer, published Wednesday, puts final Senate passage sometime this week and House passage this year as uncertain, with Democrats divided and federal funding expiring December 11 [4].

The live fight is no longer whether America builds faster. It is who gets consulted before the building starts. The substitute rewrites Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act — the part that requires federal agencies to consult Tribal Nations before approving projects that could harm historic, cultural and sacred places [2]. On Tuesday, the bill's four authors — Senators Mike Lee (R-Utah), Martin Heinrich (D-N.M.), Shelley Moore Capito (R-W.Va.) and Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.) — filed one more set of changes, S.Amdt. 7022, according to the Congressional Record [2]. It was ordered to lie on the table; it has not been offered on the floor and has not been voted on [2].

What 7022 would do: narrow the substitute's blanket exemption for federally financed projects, so financial assistance escapes Section 106 review only when the agency "does not exercise predominant control and responsibility over the subsequent use of such financial assistance or any adverse effect of the project, activity, or program on a historic property," and raise the Historic Preservation Fund's annual authorization from $200 million to $300 million [2]. Both changes track amendments filed Monday by the Senate Indian Affairs leaders, Chairman Lisa Murkowski (R-Alaska) and Vice Chairman Brian Schatz (D-Hawaii) [2]. What 7022 does not do is touch the provisions tribes have objected to most — two-year federal evaluations of tribal preservation programs, an official's "sole discretion" to outweigh preservation, a narrower definition of adverse effect, and consultation with a federal council "in lieu of" a tribal office that misses a deadline [2].

The floor heard both sides plainly. Senator Jeff Merkley (D-Ore.) said Tuesday the bill "would fundamentally change the section 106 review process" and "strips Tribes of any input on their own cultural resources" [2]. Senator John Kennedy (R-La.) answered for the builders: "We are not taking away anybody's right to sue. But you can't take forever making a decision." [2] After Monday's vote, the National Association of Tribal Historic Preservation Officers' executive director Ira L. Matt said: "It is unconscionable to then let a handful of Senators jam through a 400-page backroom deal at the eleventh hour that tramples on that hard-fought right because they decided it was the best deal they could get." [2]

The bill underneath the fight would impose deadlines on federal reviews — a two-year target for major environmental reviews against a current average of more than four years, per the White House Council of Economic Advisers — set a 150-day window for many legal challenges, and require large data centers to bear more of the grid costs their demand causes [4]. Supporters cite the CEA's estimate of $82 billion to $164 billion in added annual output and $178 to $251 off the average household electric bill; those are the administration's numbers, printed here as its claims, not as settled fact [4].

Commentary: This paper wants the permission slip torn up — a review regime that averages four-plus years is government power exercised by delay, and it taxes every builder, town and ratepayer in America. But consultation rights are not red tape in costume; they are the one moment the people who were here first get a say before a sacred place is graded. The authors filing tribal fixes the night before the vote tells you they know the difference. Watch two things today: whether 7022's language actually gets in, and whether the data-center cost rule survives. Who pays, and whose freedom gets traded — same questions, both parties, every time.
