On Oct. 12, federal agents searched the Washington, D.C., home of Rep. Ilhan Omar, a Minnesota Democrat. On Tuesday, Nov. 17, a federal judge ruled that the public gets to see part of the court record behind that search. [1][2]

Chief U.S. District Judge James E. Boasberg's minute order gives the Justice Department until Friday, Nov. 20, to hand the court, in private, every document on the sealed search-warrant docket in both redacted and unredacted form. The search-warrant affidavit is excluded, but the docket sheet is included. Unless a higher court stays the order, Boasberg will post the redacted documents on the court's website "no earlier than 5:00 p.m. on November 23, 2026." The news organizations that asked for the records can challenge the redactions by Nov. 30. The court will not publish anything redacted without talking to the government first. [1]

The case is titled In re Application of Fourteen Press Organizations for Access to Sealed Search Warrant Records. NBCUniversal is part of the media coalition, and Judicial Watch filed its own motion to unseal on Oct. 15. [2][3]

**What stays secret.** At the hearing, Boasberg agreed with the Justice Department on the biggest item. "I agree that the vast majority of the warrant affidavit should remain sealed," he said, calling full release "premature," according to NBC News. [2] MS NOW reported that the affidavit runs 67 pages. [4] He did say the legal back-and-forth over Omar's rights under the Constitution's Speech or Debate Clause should come out: "All of the legal debate about speech and debate, I don't believe that should be redacted." [2] That clause says members of Congress "shall not be questioned in any other Place" for "any Speech or Debate in either House." [5]

**What the government says, and who already said it.** Justice Department attorney Alessandra Serano asked to keep the nature of possible charges sealed. Boasberg pointed out that the President and Vice President have already talked about them in public. "We've got your superiors saying what the government is looking into," he said. Serano answered: "I had zero control over what was said." Boasberg: "That seems to be a common refrain." [2] Both NBC News and MS NOW reported that Vice President JD Vance said Omar "definitely committed immigration fraud." [2][4]

MS NOW reported that agents took Omar's phone, laptop and passport, and that a person familiar with the warrant said it listed possible immigration fraud and false statements in immigration filings. The outlet also reported that U.S. Attorney Jeanine Pirro had declined the media groups' request to unseal, arguing it could hurt the investigation. [4] No charges have been filed. Omar denies wrongdoing. She called the search "an attempt to target political enemies and weaponize federal law enforcement." [2]

The search came about three weeks before the Nov. 3 midterms, which Omar won. NBC News called it "an unusual overt investigative act against a sitting member of Congress within just weeks of the election." [2][4]

**Commentary.** Madison wrote in Federalist No. 47 that putting legislative, executive and judicial power "in the same hands" is "the very definition of tyranny." [6] An executive branch that can search a legislator's home in secret, and then let its top officials try the case in public while telling the court the details must stay sealed, is trying to have it both ways. Whether Omar broke the law is for a grand jury and a court. Whether the government can use secrecy as a shield and publicity as a weapon at the same time is a question for everyone. Boasberg's order is a small step in the right direction. The same rule should apply to every member of Congress, no matter the party, and no matter who holds the White House.
