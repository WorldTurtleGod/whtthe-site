The European Space Agency's Euclid space telescope has discovered the most distant quasar to date: a galactic core seen just 630 million years after the Big Bang, breaking Euclid's own previous record by 40 million years [1]. The quasar, formally J1504+6252 and nicknamed Dún Aonghasa, is the first identified beyond a redshift of 8, at 8.13 — seeing the universe as it was roughly 13.1 billion years ago, just 5% of its current age [1].

Follow-up observations with the W.M. Keck Observatory, the Large Binocular Telescope, Hubble, and the James Webb Space Telescope confirmed its nature, and Webb showed the quasar is powered by a black hole with a mass of approximately 360 million suns [1]. That is about 90 times more massive than the black hole at the center of the Milky Way [2].

"I think it adds to the evidence that some objects in the early universe developed remarkably quickly. Just 630 million years after the Big Bang, this black hole had already grown to about 360 million times the mass of the sun and was shining as a quasar," said astronomer Julien Wolf of the European Southern Observatory, lead author of the research published in Astronomy & Astrophysics [2].

The puzzle is the feeding rate. Dún Aonghasa is consuming material at a relatively slow rate, so it must have reached its vast mass in extremely rapid growth episodes very early in time [1]. One possibility is that it started small, from a dead first-generation star, but the observed feeding rate appears too slow to explain the mass that way; another is "direct collapse," in which a huge gas cloud collapses into a black hole born far more massive than a stellar remnant [2].

Print the caveats in full: the 360-million-sun mass is an estimate inferred from the gas around the black hole, not a direct weighing, and direct collapse is a favored explanation, not a demonstrated fact. The team says the slow feeding rate is evidence for rapid early growth episodes, not proof of the mechanism [1][2]. Models suggest even earlier quasars, 270 million to 470 million years after the Big Bang, may exist [2].

"So in short: this is the furthest quasar we have ever seen, but maybe not the furthest there is to find," said astronomer Silvia Belladitta of the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy and INAF Bologna [2].
