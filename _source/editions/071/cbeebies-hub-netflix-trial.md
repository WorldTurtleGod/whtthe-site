Hey Duggee, Numberblocks and the Teletubbies are going where the kids already are: Netflix.

The BBC and Netflix announced Tuesday a "limited trial" of a CBeebies-branded hub on Netflix starting December 2, with characters from 24 selected programs. [1] Accounts of how far it reaches differ. The BBC's version says the hub launches "in the UK" and that the partnership "will also extend globally" through a distribution deal run by BBC Studios. [1] Deadline reported that BBC Studios struck a separate one-year CBeebies content deal for Netflix in the U.S. and worldwide excluding China, while the branded hub runs for six months. [3] The Guardian reported the shows will be on Netflix globally apart from China, New Zealand and South Africa. [2] The two outlets differ on the excluded countries. Bluey is not part of it: Disney+ holds exclusive rights in most countries outside the U.K. and Australia, Deadline noted. [3] The BBC says its own iPlayer "remains the home for all CBeebies content." [1] Netflix is also committing to fund "a small number of new CBeebies commissions." [1]

The BBC is not shy about why. The deal is part of its strategy "to find audiences where they are," and it will test whether shows on someone else's platform still get credited to the BBC. [1] "While BBC iPlayer remains the home of the full CBeebies experience, viewing habits are changing," said Patricia Hidalgo, director of BBC Children's and Education. [1] Netflix's U.K. content chief Anne Mensah said CBeebies holds "a special and unrivalled place in the hearts of UK families." [1]

The numbers explain the hurry. Linear broadcast TV reached just a third of British children aged 4 to 15 in an average week last year, down from 54% in 2022, the Guardian reported. [2] More than 90% of children aged 3 to 16 say they like YouTube, Netflix and Disney's services; only 65% of child users say they like iPlayer. [2] British kids aged 4 to 15 average 61 minutes of YouTube a day at home. [2]

Then there is the money. The BBC is funded by a mandatory licence fee, £180 a year, and the Guardian reported it has lost 2.7 million fee payers since the start of the decade. [2] The paper reported that the Netflix deal "will also strengthen the argument that anyone watching a streaming service should pay the BBC's licence fee," a proposal it said the British government is expected to announce later this year. [2]

American families will see a quieter version of the story: more preschool shows on a service they already pay for. Several of these titles have appeared on Netflix before; the Guardian called this trial "an unprecedented expansion." [2]

**Commentary.** The licence fee is a charge Britons must pay by law to watch live TV or use iPlayer. Now the network it funds is admitting the audience left for services people choose to buy. The honest lesson is that viewers vote with their remotes. The worrying lesson is the one the Guardian flagged: a government that answers falling demand by trying to bill Netflix watchers for the BBC. Americans who chafe at paying for things they never chose should recognize the move. Good shows will find an audience on their own; Hey Duggee doesn't need a mandatory fee to win over toddlers.
