The building at the corner of Lewiston and Knowlesville roads, in the hamlet of Wheatville within Oakfield, New York, served the community as St. Patrick's Church from 1885 until it closed in 2008 [1][2]. Denise and Gordon Linsey, of Basom, bought it in 2013 and first opened a gift shop there, which they closed during COVID in 2020 [1][2].

They thought about selling it. Instead, this year, they finished turning it into a café — three years of work to get the building the way they wanted it: railings and a ramp added so neighbours can get in, a loft built inside as their office that may seat diners later, and the building's architectural integrity preserved, including the original tin ceiling [1][2]. Simple Pleasures Café opened August 3, serving breakfast and lunch from 6 a.m. to 2 p.m., Monday through Friday [1][2]. The news this week is not the opening — it is that the couple's three-year rebuild finally got told, in profiles published November 15 and 16 [1][2].

The division of labour is the marriage, printed on the menu. Gordon, a specialized sanitary welder for 30 years who was ready for a change, is the main cook for breakfast; Denise, who worked 20 years in sales and raised their son, does most of the lunches [1][2]. Gordon: "I've always liked to bake, grilling and smoking meats" [1][2]. The menu is simple and priced like it means it: breakfast sandwiches, eggs, hashbrowns, bagels and avocado toast; wraps, deli sandwiches, melts, salads and a daily homemade soup; baked goods from Hans's Bakery in Medina; and Eastern Star Roasters coffee from Avon, which the café is the only coffee shop in Genesee County to carry [1][2]. A limited line of gift items and David's specialty teas survived from the shop days, and there is a corner with stuffed easy chairs and a TV for sitting and talking, plus a small kids' area [1][2].

Denise's verdict on the room is the whole story in one line: "The locals love that we're here" [1][2].

Commentary: No program did this. No organisation absorbed a landmark, and no grant rebuilt it — a welder and a sales worker spent three years of their own labour on a building their town had already lost once, kept its tin ceiling, added a ramp so everyone could get in, and opened the doors at 6 a.m. That is common-man culture in its working clothes, and it is why this desk keeps telling these stories: the country is still full of people who fix the place themselves.
