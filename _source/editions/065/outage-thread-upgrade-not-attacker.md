A week of big systems fell down in public: Microsoft 365, the Federal Aviation Administration's network, Workday, Chase, United, Apple's services, and the National Security Agency's classified internal network. Readers asked the obvious question — what's the thread? This paper chased it. Here is the graded answer.

Documented: there is no coordinated attacker in the record. National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross told Semafor on November 16: "No, this is not any sort of coordinated, nefarious cyberattack from a malicious actor. What we are seeing, and frankly I'd expect to see more of it, are service disruptions coming from updating and modernizing legacy systems" [1]. Every stated cause since has been different, internal, and mundane in the same way: a change made to a live system that everything else depends on.

Microsoft 365, November 12–14: a latent defect in a fix prepared for its networking gateway servers, rolling out since July, caused lock contention under load while about 14 percent of gateway capacity was already out for an unrelated issue; at the lowest point more than 80 percent of servers were unresponsive, and service was restored November 13 [1][2]. The FAA ground stop, Friday, November 13, from 3:54 to 8:15 p.m. ET: a router installed at El Dorado, Arkansas, during copper-to-fiber work flooded the network with about 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds, a second router at Amarillo, Texas, also flooded, and 17 of 22 en route centers were hit [1][3]. Workday locked users out after a problem found while testing its backup systems left data inconsistent [1]. Chase called its November 16 failure "a temporary issue related to a software bug in on-premise hardware systems," with no evidence of a cyber cause [1]. Apple, on November 17, said it plainly in its own statement: "Many of our systems are back up and running after an internal update caused some outages this morning" [4].

The FAA story has since moved from pause to retreat, and that is the fresh news. The Associated Press reports the FAA "paused new installations of Juniper routers and disabled the functionality of all existing ones on Friday after technicians linked the problems to a new router in El Dorado, Arkansas," and is "relying on older Cisco routers that have been in place for 25 years" while it investigates [5]. Only about 400 of more than 3,600 planned routers had been installed [5]. HPE, which owns Juniper, says the device "functioned as it was configured to by the installer" — reported, not settled [5]. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy's position has not changed: "We've been able to recreate what happened in El Dorado. But the root cause of what happened, we don't have clarity yet" [5][6]. Testing continues at an L3Harris laboratory in Florida with the FAA, Cisco and the FBI on site, and the pause remains in effect with no restart date [6].

Two soft spots stay open, labeled as open — not as proof of anything. First, that missing FAA root cause: the agency can reproduce the flood and still cannot say why it started. Second, the NSA. NSA Net, the agency's classified internal network, went down the morning of November 17; the NSA called it "temporary degradation during scheduled routine maintenance" and said mission-critical capabilities remained operational [1][7]. Since then the agency has given no all-clear statement that this paper could find. CNN reported November 18 that restoration patching was going "slower than expected," that Maryland non-essential day-shift personnel were told not to report unless directed, and that the Georgia cryptologic center lost NSANet contact with deployed colleagues and worked around it [8]. Secondary reports conflict — some say the network was back up Wednesday, others note no agency statement confirms a full restoration — so this paper prints the conflict, not a resolution [8]. No new nationwide outage was verified for Wednesday, November 18.

And the attacks that were real were separate. Optimizely, Ipsos, Infoblox and Equinor disclosed actual cyber incidents the same week; no official or company has tied any of them to the outages above, and neither will this paper [1].

Commentary: Now apply the two questions. Who pays? The administration has sent Congress a request for $50.1 billion — $10.1 billion of it for air traffic control modernization, the rest for airports, terminals, new centers and towers — to fix a system whose latest failure it still cannot explain [5]. What freedom does it cost? Less obvious, and worth watching: every unexplained outage becomes an argument for emergency power and emergency money, granted fast and audited later. The thread is not a hidden hand. It is a country running critical systems old enough that changing them is the most dangerous thing you can do to them — and a government that reaches for $50 billion before it can tell you why the last change broke.
