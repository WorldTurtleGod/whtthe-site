The most important sentence in Washington this week was five words long: "Commerce has the pen."

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said it on CNBC's Squawk Box on Friday, Nov. 13, and he did not say it as a compliment. Megaspeed International, the Singapore-based Nvidia customer at the center of the scandal that brought down Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, has been under Commerce Department investigation since 2024 on suspicion of funneling restricted American AI chips to China. Commerce has never put it on the Entity List — the export blacklist that bars U.S. suppliers from selling to listed firms without a license. [1]

Bessent told CNBC exactly why not, and who wanted it done: "Treasury, War, USTR and State were in favor of putting Megaspeed on the entity list for months." Four departments of the United States government reached the same conclusion about a suspected chip pipeline to Beijing. The one department with the legal authority to act sat on it. "Commerce… has the pen… and you will have to ask them about their internal process," Bessent said. [1][2][3]

He went further, and this is the part that should end careers. Bessent suggested the inaction defied the President himself: "I am frustrated when President Trump made it very clear that China should not receive any Nvidia chips above H100s. And so any leakage is very frustrating because it hurts our great companies in the AI race, gives the Chinese an advantage." Asked whether the processors reached China through Megaspeed, he conceded: "I think that there is a lot of leakage in the system, and we are constantly working to close that down." [1][3]

Understand what the Entity List is, because it is the whole story. The power to regulate commerce with foreign nations belongs to Congress. Congress delegated the blacklist pen to the Commerce Department. That pen is not a veto and it is not a pocket to keep directives in — it is an instrument for executing the law and the President's stated policy. When four agencies say a company is a national-security risk, when the Senate Intelligence Committee chairman — Republican Tom Cotton — has personally asked the secretary to investigate that company, and when the President has publicly drawn the line at H100s, a secretary who does nothing is not exercising judgment. He is nullifying the policy of the government he serves, one unreturned decision at a time. [1]

Capitol Hill has noticed, on both sides. House Foreign Affairs Committee Democrats have written to Lutnick demanding to know what he and his export-control chief, Jeffrey Kessler, knew about Megaspeed, and when. Senate Democrats have directed him to preserve documents and records on Nvidia's advanced-chip exports. The Republican chairs of three House committees — Foreign Affairs, Intelligence, and the China select committee — have asked Huang and Nvidia's general counsel for closed-door interviews next month, and Kessler has been asked to appear at a hearing. Lutnick himself is set to face the Senate Banking Committee on Dec. 10. [1][4]

Republicans are not protecting him. "This news is as concerning as it gets," Sen. Jim Banks, a Trump ally, said Thursday on Fox News. "When you're in a war with another country, you don't give them your best technologies." Sen. Thom Tillis was blunter: "Shame on Lutnick for allowing that exposure to happen for the president. Raises a question about what other ones are out there." [1]

CNN reported Sunday that some of Trump's advisers, reeling from Huang's resignation, have begun discussing whether Lutnick should be dismissed — even as the White House's public line, issued Thursday, holds that Trump "maintains complete confidence in the secretary and his ability to carry out the position as the most transformative Commerce Secretary in modern history." Both cannot stay true for long. [1][5]

Commerce, for its part, is denying the record. A department spokesperson denied that Bessent ever confronted Lutnick over the delay, and on Saturday, Nov. 14, Commerce told Axios: "The reports you have issued are inaccurate and false and literally misstate the topic, creating a false impression." But the department has not announced the one action that would end the argument — putting Megaspeed on the list — and Megaspeed still does not appear on it. [4]

The stakes walk into a summit this week: Trump is preparing to meet Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, the hub of China's tech industry, with the chip question hanging over the table. [1]

Here is the test, and it is simple. A government that cannot blacklist one Singapore company suspected of feeding its rival's AI program — after four of its own departments, its Treasury secretary, and its Senate Intelligence chairman have all said to do it — does not have an export-control policy. It has a press release. Either the pen moves, or the President should hand it to someone who will use it.
