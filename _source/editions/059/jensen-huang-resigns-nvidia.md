Jensen Huang is out at Nvidia.

The company announced Thursday, Nov. 12, that Huang resigned as president, chief executive, and board member, effective immediately. Chief financial officer Colette Kress takes over as interim CEO while keeping her CFO job, and former Synopsys chief Harvey Jones becomes Nvidia's first-ever board chairman. [1][2]

Huang co-founded Nvidia in 1993 and ran it for 33 years — the longest-serving founder-CEO in the chip business, and the man who turned a graphics-card company into the most valuable company on earth and the beating heart of the AI boom. He is also its fourth-largest investor, holding about 3.61 percent of the stock. [1] The market reacted the way markets react when the indispensable man leaves: shares fell more than 10 percent in premarket trading, slumped as much as 14 percent during the session, and closed down 4.3 percent. [1]

The resignation caps a week that moved fast. On Nov. 8, Huang admitted he had been unfaithful to his wife, and Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation into reports that he had been targeted by a foreign intelligence operation. The reports behind it: Huang had an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based cloud company Megaspeed, whom U.S. authorities have concluded was a Chinese intelligence asset. Beijing denies she is a spy. Then on Nov. 11, the New York Times reported Huang also had an affair with Kimberly Powell, Nvidia's vice president of healthcare, who reported directly to him. Nvidia's board met Wednesday to consider his ouster. [1][2]

The company's announcement gave no reason for the resignation. It did not have to. A founder-CEO does not walk away from a $4 trillion company in the middle of the biggest boom in its history because he felt like it. [1][2]

President Trump, who has leaned on Huang as an ally on chip-export policy, said Sunday that Huang had "done an amazing job for our country" and that Huang told him he had no idea the woman was a spy. [1] That is the defense Huang has offered, and it may be true. But the Powell affair — a subordinate, inside his own company — is the part no defense about spies can cover. Boards can survive a CEO's private life. They cannot survive a CEO who mixes it with the org chart, and they certainly cannot when the other affair hands America's adversaries a lever on the most strategically important company in the country.

What happens now is the question nobody can answer yet. Nvidia's chips are the oil of the AI boom, and Huang was not just running the company — he was its product vision, its dealmaker, and its public face, mobbed by fans in Taiwan and South Korea who asked him to sign books and posters. [1] Kress is a respected operator who has challenged Huang's bets before, but she is interim, and the board is now searching inside and outside the company for a permanent successor. [1][3] Nvidia reports third-quarter earnings Tuesday, Nov. 17, as scheduled — and for the first time in 33 years, someone other than Jensen Huang will have to explain what Nvidia is. [2]
