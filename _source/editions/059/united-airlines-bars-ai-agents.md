The tech industry has spent the year promising a robot butler that books your trips. United Airlines just told the butler to wait in the lobby.

On Wednesday, Nov. 11, United said it would restrict AI agents' access to its digital platforms, citing data security. [1] The carrier's own help page spells it out: if a third-party AI agent, bot, browser extension or browser feature "searches, books, buys, manages or collects information on united.com or the United app for you, and United hasn't given permission, it's unauthorized." [2]

The rule bites even when the traveler gave the AI permission. "Just because you've given access to AI assistants doesn't mean United will permit an AI assistant to transact with United," the airline says. [2] And it reaches past United's own website. Travel agencies and booking platforms cannot use third-party AI agents to search, book or manage United travel, directly or indirectly, unless United allows it. [2]

InsideFlyer reports that United wrote the ban into its contract rules on Nov. 11: Rule 6.J.6 covers using AI agents to make, change or service a ticket, and Rule 5.H gives the airline the authority to cancel reservations handled that way. [3] Bookings an agent made before Nov. 11 will not be canceled, United says. [2]

What is still allowed is window shopping. An AI tool can show United's fares and seats, and the customer can then finish the purchase themselves. [3] United even suggests safe questions to ask a chatbot, such as where to go in the Caribbean in January to avoid crowds, and warns against handing over MileagePlus numbers, passwords or confirmation codes. [2] Assistive technology for customers with disabilities is not affected. [2]

United gives three main reasons. Sharing account logins with an AI could expose personal information and lead to unintended purchases, account changes or the unauthorized use of frequent-flyer miles. [1][2] And some steps are supposed to be done by a person: check-in "requires human acknowledgment that bags do not contain banned substances like explosives," so an AI doing it automatically could violate FAA and DOT rules, the airline says. [2][4]

United is not alone in drawing lines. CNBC notes that Amazon has blocked Meta's AI agent Muse while Walmart partnered with it, and that Expedia and Booking are working with OpenAI. [1] United says it does not work with any third-party AI agents "yet," and hopes to set up "standardized, secure technology integrations" with privacy, authorization and accountability protections first. [2]

**Commentary.** There is an old common-law idea that you can hire an agent to act for you, and the people you deal with have to respect it. United's position is that a software agent is not that kind of agent until the airline says so. Some of its reasons are hard to argue with: a bot should not be the one promising federal regulators there is nothing dangerous in your luggage. But "we will decide which robots you may hire" is also a handy way for a big company to keep the customer on its own website, where it sells the upgrades. Travelers will have to decide which of those two things this really is.
