The oldest fact about the federal courts is that they cannot pay their own electric bill. On Thursday night, Justice Samuel Alito said it out loud.

"Congress can turn off the lights at the Supreme Court and stop everything from happening if they want to defund their activities," Alito told the Federalist Society's annual Antonin Scalia Memorial Dinner in Washington. "And there are many, many, many other things that they can do, or at least try to do." [1]

"So, it is important to preserve the independence of the judiciary," he went on. "If that is eliminated, then the Constitution as adopted in 1789 will no longer be the same." [1]

The timing gave the remark its edge. The dinner came a week after Democrats won control of both houses of Congress. Alito did not mention the election or the coming change in power, but The Hill notes that Democrats have talked before about using the power of the purse as leverage against the court's conservative majority. Over the summer, Justices Elena Kagan and Amy Coney Barrett made a rare trip to Capitol Hill to argue for the judiciary's budget and more security money as threats against judges rose. [1]

Alito called the judiciary the least powerful branch and said it is "vulnerable" to both Congress and the executive. He did not spare his own side of the bench, though. Using an image of a hand grenade, a 500-pound bomb and a nuclear weapon, he said the courts are the smallest of the three, but still dangerous. He also scolded lower-court judges for sometimes issuing "unjustified" orders blocking presidents' policies. "They are dedicated and they are skillful, but they're not perfect and they make mistakes," he said. "And in some instances, I think they have gone far astray in abuse of their powers." [1] More than 1,000 conservative lawyers were in the room. [1]

**The old argument underneath.** None of this would have surprised Alexander Hamilton. In Federalist No. 78 he wrote that the judiciary "will always be the least dangerous to the political rights of the Constitution," because it "has no influence over either the sword or the purse." It has, he said, "neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment." [2]

Hamilton knew what followed from that. In Federalist No. 79 he put it in one line: "A power over a man's subsistence amounts to a power over his will." [3] So the framers locked one door. Article III says federal judges "shall, at stated Times, receive for their Services, a Compensation, which shall not be diminished during their Continuance in Office." [4] Congress cannot cut a sitting judge's pay. What the text does not lock is everything around the judge: the staff, the security, the building, the lights. Those come out of annual appropriations, which is the door Alito was pointing at.

**Commentary.** There is a fair complaint on each side here, and the founders would have wanted both heard. The purse belongs to Congress, and an elected Congress asking hard questions about how courts spend public money is the system working, not failing. But a Congress that starves a court because it dislikes the court's rulings is using the purse as a gag, and that is the very "power over his will" Hamilton warned about. Alito's own point cuts the other way, too: a judiciary that wants to be left alone by the political branches does best when it stays out of their business, and his scolding of lower courts suggests he knows it. Separation of powers was never meant to make any branch comfortable. It was meant to keep each one from getting its hands on the others' throats.
