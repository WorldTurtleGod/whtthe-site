On Sunday, November 8, the chief executive of the world's most valuable chip company sent an email to his tens of thousands of employees and admitted he had cheated on his wife of 40 years. Jensen Huang, 63, wrote: "I owe my family and all of you an apology. I was unfaithful to my wife. My actions hurt her and our children. There is no excuse for what I did. I am deeply sorry." Five paragraphs later, he tied the affair himself to "the national security concerns raised in news reports" — and said Nvidia's board had opened an independent investigation. [1][2]

The woman at the center of it is Alice Huang — also known as Huang Le — a Chinese citizen and an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore-based data-centre company. She is not related to Jensen Huang. The New York Times reported on October 29 that U.S. officials had concluded she was working for China, and a classified memo reported by the Times said China's Ministry of State Security recruited her to gather intelligence on Jensen Huang and to nudge Nvidia to lobby for China to get its most advanced chips. Megaspeed has bought more than $4.8 billion worth of Nvidia chips in the past two years, and the U.S. and Singapore have both previously investigated the firm over suspicions it was illegally funnelling chips to China. [1][2]

None of the spying claims has been proven. China has called them "groundless speculation." Alice Huang has not commented publicly. Nvidia's board said it has "no reason to believe that either national security or NVIDIA secrets were compromised," while promising a full inquiry led by independent directors with outside counsel — and Huang says he will cooperate fully. [1][3]

Washington is not waiting for the inquiry. Three Republican committee chairmen — Brian Mast (Foreign Affairs), John Moolenaar (China), and Rick Crawford (Intelligence) — asked Huang for a closed-door briefing on November 18, the day after Nvidia's next earnings report. Representative Michael McCaul called the affair "a classic move from the CCP's playbook" and called for an immediate pause on advanced chip shipments to China. President Trump said on Monday that the FBI may get involved. [2]

Strip the scandal down and two things are true at once. A powerful man's private failure is his own business — until the person on the other side of it is, by the U.S. government's own assessment, an asset of a foreign intelligence service trying to get at the chips that run the AI race. That is why this is not a gossip story. The affair is admitted. The compromise is unproven. And the briefing on the 18th is where the country finds out which of those two facts matters more.
