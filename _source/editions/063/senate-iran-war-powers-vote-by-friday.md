The U.S. war with Iran began on Feb. 28, 2026, when President Trump ordered air strikes inside Iran's territory without congressional authorization or consultation. That date is in the text of S.J.Res. 118, introduced in the Senate a week later, and in a later resolution that records the President's March 2 notice to Congress under the War Powers Resolution. [8][9] U.S. Central Command said the same day that it had commenced Operation Epic Fury at the President's direction, with strikes beginning at 1:15 a.m. Eastern. [10] The conflict is now eight months and nearly three weeks old, approaching its ninth month, and Congress has still never voted to authorize it. This week, the Senate has to go on the record anyway.

The measure is H.Con.Res. 93, a concurrent resolution introduced April 28 by Rep. Seth Moulton of Massachusetts. It invokes section 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution and directs the President "to remove United States Armed Forces from hostilities against the Islamic Republic of Iran," except for forces needed to defend the United States or an ally or partner from imminent attack, "unless explicitly authorized by a declaration of war or a specific congressional authorization for use of military force against Iran." [1]

The House passed it Sept. 15 by 220–204. [2] Factrail reports that all voting Democrats backed it, joined by seven Republicans. The Senate received it the next day and sent it to the Foreign Relations Committee, where it sat. [2][3] According to Factrail, Senate leaders agreed on Nov. 9 by unanimous consent to pull it out of committee no later than Friday, Nov. 20, with up to six hours of debate followed by a vote. The Senate's own floor log restated that schedule on Monday night. [3][4]

The vote lands two weeks after an election in which this war was a central issue. Democrats won both chambers on Nov. 3 and take over Jan. 3. Hakeem Jeffries, the top House Democrat, has said: "We need to end the reckless and costly war of choice in Iran. That's been a commitment that we've made." [5] A Reuters/Ipsos poll of 1,705 adults found 71% of Americans want Congress to pass legislation ending the war, including 93% of Democrats and 51% of Republicans. [6]

Be clear about what this vote can and cannot do. A concurrent resolution never goes to the President's desk, and Factrail notes that its legal force is disputed; the White House has leaned on the President's Article II powers as commander in chief. A yes vote would not cancel money already appropriated, and a no vote would not authorize anything. The real leverage is the purse: according to Factrail, the current stopgap funding law, Public Law 119-103, runs out Dec. 11. [3]

What the vote does do is end the hiding. For two months, every senator could say the matter was "in committee." By Friday, each of them will have a name next to a yes or a no on whether the President may keep fighting a war Congress never declared.

**Commentary.** The Constitution does not give the power to start a war to the President. Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power "to declare War," and Madison wrote that the Constitution "supposes, what the History of all Govts demonstrates, that the Ex. is the branch of power most interested in war, & most prone to it. It has accordingly with studied care, vested the question of war in the Legisl." [7] Eight-plus months and no authorization is not a gray area. It is Congress letting the executive take a power the founders deliberately kept from it. Senators of both parties have spent years complaining about executive overreach when the other side holds the White House. This week costs them nothing but courage. A senator who will not vote to reclaim the war power has no business complaining when the next President, of either party, uses it.
