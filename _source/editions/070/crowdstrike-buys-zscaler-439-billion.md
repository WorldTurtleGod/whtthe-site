CrowdStrike said Monday it will buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal that values the company at about $43.9 billion, the companies announced November 23 [1][2]. Zscaler shareholders will receive 1.135 CrowdStrike shares for each Zscaler share — an exchange ratio that implies a value of about $260.41 per Zscaler share, a 34% premium to Zscaler's last close [2]. Existing CrowdStrike shareholders will own about 84.1% of the combined company after the transaction closes [2].

The logic, as the companies tell it, is consolidation. CrowdStrike secures the devices on a company's network — computers, laptops, phones. Zscaler, based in San Jose, provides cloud cybersecurity to about 8,000 enterprise companies, built on zero-trust technology: employees must authenticate every time they access an internal system, so a stolen password alone does not open the network [1]. Put together, the companies say they can offer "superior protection" against data breaches and help customers safely adopt AI [1]. Reuters reports the combination "will create one of the world's largest pure-play cybersecurity providers as enterprise customers seek to reduce the number of security vendors they use, favoring providers that can deliver multiple products through a single relationship" [2].

The numbers behind the pitch: the companies expect to start with more than $10 billion in combined annual recurring revenue and target $1 billion of annualized revenue synergies [2]. The deal is subject to regulatory approval and is expected to close around this time next year [1].

The market's first verdict split the two sides. Zscaler shares jumped more than 13.4% in premarket trading; CrowdStrike fell 5% [2]. That is the usual shape of these announcements — the bought company's shareholders get paid, the buyer's shareholders get the integration risk — but the size here makes the risk real. Forty-four billion dollars in stock for a company whose whole pitch is that the network perimeter is dead and every login must be verified is a bet that the future of cybersecurity is one vendor selling the whole stack, and that customers would rather buy it all from CrowdStrike than assemble it themselves. The regulators get a say first, and the customers get the final one: if the combined product is genuinely better, the premium pays for itself; if it is just bigger, the 5% drop on day one was the market pricing the hangover early.
