On Monday, November 23, Nvidia's board of directors unanimously voted to reinstate Jensen Huang as president and CEO, effective immediately — eleven days after he resigned [1][2]. Huang stepped down on November 12 after revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with a businesswoman whom U.S. authorities suspect of spying for China, an allegation Beijing denies [1]. The board said Monday it was "satisfied with Huang's candor and leadership ability and, after receiving input from senior US government officials, saw no national security risk from his return" [1].

The reversal came after President Donald Trump personally lobbied the board. Trump said he spoke "directly and indirectly" with Nvidia's directors to convince them to reappoint Huang. "I spoke to them, let me put it that way," he told CNBC. "I convinced them. I made the deal" [2]. On Truth Social he wrote that the government had "no reason to believe that Jensen's return to NVIDIA poses any risk to National Security" and no evidence Huang had engaged in unlawful conduct [3]. Trump also told CNBC that the stock market's reaction to Huang's resignation "probably" factored into his push [3].

The market answered fast. Nvidia shares rose 32% in early afternoon trading Monday, "equivalent to more than $900 billion in market capitalization added back," and the Nasdaq 100 jumped more than 6% [3]. A petition calling for Huang's reinstatement had drawn more than 180,000 signatures, including one from Elon Musk [1]. The board's own chairman, Harvey Jones, had said just last week that the company would not rehire Huang [1].

Congress is not letting it rest. Sen. Elizabeth Warren, the top Democrat on the Senate Banking Committee, said: "The reckless decision to bring Jensen Huang back as CEO does not change the fact that Mr. Huang, NVIDIA executives, and the company's board still owe Congress answers about the company's compliance with U.S. export controls" [2]. She added: "The Trump Administration also needs to explain to Congress what 'input' was given and by which senior officials in the United States government to support his reinstatement" [2].

Sen. Adam Schiff said: "Congress must follow the trail of who financially benefited from the sales and diversion of advanced chips to China, what the administration and NVIDIA's CEO knew about China's effort to illicitly acquire this technology, and when they knew it" [2]. Senate Democratic Leader Chuck Schumer wrote that Trump "is putting his own financial interests and the needs of his billionaire buddies before our national security," and pledged Democrats "will wield their full power to investigate any wrongdoing by the Trump Administration, Jensen Huang, other NVIDIA executives, and anyone selling out our national security for financial gain" [2]. Rep. Hakeem Jeffries, poised to become the next House speaker, called the reinstatement "indefensible, irresponsible and incomprehensible" and promised Congress "will not be deterred from conducting a swift, thorough and wide-ranging investigation" [2].

Republicans had questions too. Sen. John Curtis of Utah said: "Nvidia's board says it relied on input from senior U.S. officials to conclude that bringing Jensen Huang back posed no national security risk. Who in our government weighed in? What did they tell the board? And on what basis did the board conclude there was no risk?" [2]. Sen. John Cornyn of Texas asked: "No national security risk? Based on what?" [2]. House Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Brian Mast said he expects Huang to appear before members of Congress next week for a transcribed interview about how he may have jeopardized U.S. national security [2]. Rep. Don Bacon of Nebraska wrote on X that Huang was "compromised and exploited by a Chinese spy" and that "Nvidia should have moved on without Huang and ignored the Administration's inexplicable desire to save Huang" [2].

Huang is due before three House committees for closed-door questioning on December 1, and before the Senate Banking Committee on December 3 [2][4]. The reinstatement also came while Nvidia's own internal probe into Huang's affair with the suspected spy is still ongoing [2]. California's attorney general said Monday that the reinstatement "does not diminish the need for a probe into what Nvidia told investors about its China ties" [3].

Grade it as it sits: the reinstatement, the board's statement, the president's lobbying, the market move, and the congressional reaction are all documented and on the record [1][2][3]. The substance of the national-security question — what Huang knew, what chips went where — is not settled; it is the subject of an open board probe, an open California inquiry, and hearings that have not happened yet. This paper prints the alleged spy connection as an allegation, not a fact, because U.S. authorities suspect it and Beijing denies it [1].

Commentary: Apply the same standard in both directions. A president leaning on a private company's board to reinstall its chief executive is executive power reaching into a boardroom — and it worked in eleven days, after the board had publicly said no. That is power growing, and it deserves the question Senator Curtis asked: who in the government weighed in, and what did they say? But the other side of the ledger is real too: a Congress that will hold the majority in January promising a "wide-ranging investigation" into the president's business dealings is legislative power growing, and it will deserve the same question when it starts issuing subpoenas. Who pays? Start with the shareholders and the millions of Americans whose retirement accounts ride the AI trade — a single man's job moved $900 billion in an afternoon. What freedom does it cost? The freedom of a company to choose its own leadership without the White House in the room — and the freedom of the public to know whether the man running America's most important chip company was compromised by a foreign intelligence service. Neither question has an answer yet. December 1 and 3 will be the first chance to get one.
