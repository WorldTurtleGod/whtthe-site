One arm of the federal government says the world's most widely used weed killer is not likely to cause cancer. Another arm's scientists just reviewed decades of evidence and found a link to blood cancers.

On Friday, Nov. 20, seven researchers led by the Report on Carcinogens group at the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences (NIEHS), part of the National Institutes of Health, posted a preprint titled "Evaluating Evidence of Glyphosate-Based Herbicides and Lymphohematopoietic Cancers from Epidemiologic Studies: A Systematic Review." [1][3] Glyphosate is the active ingredient in Bayer's Roundup. Lymphohematopoietic cancers are cancers of the blood and lymph system.

"The current body of epidemiology studies found a positive association between GBH exposure and LHCs," the authors wrote, according to MLQ.ai, using their shorthand for glyphosate-based herbicides and those cancers. [3] The review covered 24 independent study populations on non-Hodgkin lymphoma, 20 on multiple myeloma, seven on Hodgkin lymphoma and two on leukemia. Every study reviewed had at least one positive association, mostly for non-Hodgkin lymphoma or one of its subtypes. The authors' bias analysis found that bias "did not reasonably explain the positive findings," MLQ.ai reported. [3]

The authors were also clear about the limits. Few studies covered leukemia, and "inconsistency in the types of NHL suggests that chance cannot be reasonably be ruled out," they wrote, according to MLQ.ai. [3] The paper is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed. [1][3]

It wasn't the only review. The Washington Post reported that NIH scientists released two analyses. One found an association between glyphosate herbicides and blood cancers in people. The other found that glyphosate caused several types of tumors in laboratory animals. [2] MLQ.ai reported that the animal review and a commentary tying the two together were submitted as companion papers. [3]

The work was ordered from the top. NIEHS says its Division of Translational Toxicology got a request in late 2025 from the Department of Health and Human Services, run by Robert F. Kennedy Jr., to evaluate whether glyphosate exposure is linked to cancer or to male reproductive health problems. The agency set up separate systematic reviews for each. [4] The reproductive-health review has not been released.

That puts HHS scientists at odds with the EPA. For years the EPA has said glyphosate is "not likely" to cause cancer, the Post reported, and the findings could put pressure on the agency to change that position. [2][3] Glyphosate's cancer question has divided regulators for a decade. The World Health Organization's cancer agency, IARC, classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic to humans" in 2015, while U.S. and European regulators did not reach that conclusion. [5]

Here is what the review does not say. It does not say glyphosate causes cancer in people. It reports an association in observational studies, mostly of farmers and pesticide applicators with heavy exposure. The authors rate parts of the evidence as uncertain, and it has yet to clear peer review.

**Commentary.** Don't treat this as a verdict, and don't wave it away. The useful thing is that the evidence is public. Anyone can read the preprint, check the studies and argue with the method before a regulator acts on it. That is how government science should work. The worse outcome would be one agency quietly overruling another behind closed doors, in either direction, with a Fortune 500 company's legal exposure riding on the result. Peer review should go fast, the reproductive-health review should come out too, and the EPA should explain in writing whatever it decides.
