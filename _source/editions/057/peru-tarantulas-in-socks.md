Travelers have always carried strange cargo out of distant lands: spices, seeds, feathers, relics. This month three of them tried to fly out of Peru with spiders.

Peru's national forestry and wildlife service, SERFOR, said on November 4 that its staff and customs agents had seized more than 3,000 tarantulas from the luggage of two German citizens and one Polish citizen at Jorge Chávez International Airport in Lima. The three were bound for Germany and were caught at the check-in counter, about to board. [1]

The spiders were packed in labeled socks inside three suitcases, at every stage of life. "Concealed in socks inside three suitcases, we found the tarantulas in different stages of life, from eggs and hatchlings to adults," said Cora Suárez, a SERFOR wildlife control technician. "The traffickers made a meticulous selection to include different species, some of which have not yet been described by science." [1][2]

The haul came from regions across Peru, including Junín, Loreto, Cajamarca, Cusco, and La Libertad. It included members of the genus Pamphobeteus, the blue bird eater Thrixopelma lagunas, Antikuna and Avicularia spiders not yet described by science, and the Bolivian blue leg bird eater, Pamphobeteus antinous, which Peru lists as vulnerable. Mongabay notes that none of the seized species is protected under CITES, the international wildlife-trade treaty. Peru protects them under its own law. [1][2]

Then the count went up. Peruvian prosecutors later described about 12,000 tarantulas. Infobae reports that the National Police, after a final count, put the total at 12,796 animals, and that the three suspects were held under a seven-day preliminary detention order while investigators look into a possible international trafficking network. [3][4]

SERFOR says that, according to scientific publications, one of the detainees appears to be a recognized tarantula researcher. Authorities have not named that person. [1][2] Police told the Peruvian TV program 24 Horas that the operation began with a tip from a protected witness, that Peruvians, possibly including tour guides, may have collected and moved the spiders, and that the suspects' social media accounts listed tarantulas at prices from $25 up to $300 or $400. [3]

What the spiders are worth depends on who you ask. Infobae puts the whole lot at around half a million euros on the European market. Mongabay found individual Pamphobeteus selling online for as much as 60 pounds (about $80). [2][3] Either way, it is a lot of money to leave rustling in a sock.

SERFOR says the spiders will go to an authorized breeding center once they have been identified. [1] Old travelers' tales are full of exotic creatures smuggled home in a trunk. This one has a government press release, a police tally to the last spider, and a sock drawer no one will ever want to open.
