The newest idea in AI regulation is not a law. It is a court order with a date on it: nothing smarter than November 12, 2026, ever again, until a judge says otherwise.

On Thursday, Nov. 12, four organizations — the Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, the Heat Initiative and The Alliance for Secure AI — filed a 63-page complaint in San Francisco Superior Court against Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceX's AI business, the developer of Grok. Seven legal entities are named in total. The suit brings a single cause of action under California's Unfair Competition Law, Business and Professions Code section 17200, alleging a public nuisance and unfair practice. It seeks no damages — injunctions and attorneys' fees only. [1][2]

The two prayers tell you the ambition. The first asks for an immediate injunction barring the labs from developing or deploying systems "more capable than their most capable systems as of the date of this Complaint." The second asks to bar using AI to autonomously conduct or direct research and development of more capable systems — the recursive self-improvement loop, where the AI helps build its successor. Existing ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini would not necessarily shut down. The target is the next model. [1]

The plaintiffs' lawyer is Matthew Platkin, a former New Jersey attorney general, and his argument is that the labs have already made his case for him: "It is patently illegal for companies to develop and release products they know are unsafe in any respect, let alone have the potential to exterminate the human race." Public Citizen's co-president Robert Weissman framed the ask the same way: "Our lawsuit asks the court to apply existing law and find the AI corporations' reckless practices unlawful and freeze further AI development." The complaint leans heavily on the labs' own filings — including Anthropic's Nov. 10 S-1 amendment warning of "the scale of harm that could result from a catastrophic AI-related incident," filed two days before the suit, as Anthropic prepares to price its IPO on Wednesday, Nov. 18. [1]

Now the straight part, because this paper's stance does not upgrade evidence. This is a filing, not a ruling. There is no hearing date, no response from the labs on file, and no freeze in effect. As of Sunday, Nov. 15 reporting, Meta had declined to comment and the other defendants had not responded. The complaint never defines "more capable," and standing — whether these plaintiffs can bring this claim at all — will likely be the first fight. A single state unfair-competition claim freezing five companies' research is, on the reporting's own assessment, a long shot. [1]

But notice what kind of power is being requested, and by whom. Not Congress, after a debate the country can watch. A state court, at the request of four nonprofits, deciding what private researchers may build — with the cutoff set not by a standard anyone voted on but by a calendar date. If frontier AI is dangerous, that is an argument to take to the people's representatives in the open. A freeze by injunction asks one judge to do quietly what no legislature has done at all. The labs' own alarmist filings invited this suit. That does not make the suit's remedy a free one.
