Albums usually save the hit for the front. U2 saved the goodbye for the back.

The band released Carnaval de Luz on Friday, its 16th studio album and its first full-length record of all-new material in nine years. [1][2] The last of its 12 songs is "Torn," a duet between Bono and Dolly Parton. Parton, who died in August at 80, recorded her part in June. It is her final recording. [1][2]

"When she got ill," Bono told Rolling Stone, "she got out of her bed to sing this song. And you can hear that. And I can't even listen to it now." [1]

The song took a winding road. Bono first approached Parton for an Irish-roots side project that is still in progress, and when he played her sketches over the phone, she told him, "That's a little too Irish." She sent back a song of her own called "Torn," about a woman choosing between two men. Bono and Snow Patrol's Johnny McDaid rewrote it around a mother watching her family split apart, and Parton agreed to sing the new version. She shares the writing credit. [1]

The chorus carries the change: "Torn apart these loving hearts that beat and break the same / We share a house divided and the blood that fills our veins." In one verse, Parton sings, "Nothing hurts a mother more than a family at war." [1] Daniel Lanois added pedal steel, and producer Garret "Jacknife" Lee says Parton approved the final mix and "loved it." "She was so proud of her vocal and the recording," he said. [1]

Variety's Chris Willman calls the album "definitely imperfect but also rewarding," a record loosely themed around Latin America's pre-Lent carnival celebrations that rocks more like '90s U2 than '80s U2. But he saves his warmest words for the closer, where Parton "does some actual belting in the final moments," and tells readers to "save all the tissues" for it. [2]

**Commentary.** The lyric reaches for "a house divided," and Bono is not shy about where he aims it. "People say in the U.S. now, people are scared to go into Thanksgiving," he told Rolling Stone. [1] The Edge described Parton as standing in "the radical center," beloved by both sides, and said her secret weapon was humor: "no tyrant has a defense against humor, turns out." [1] It is a fitting last word for a woman who spent a career making people who agreed on nothing else agree on her.
