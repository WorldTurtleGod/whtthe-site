On Friday afternoon, in a short list of orders from their private conference, the justices of the Supreme Court said yes to a case that will decide whether a president can switch off asylum at the U.S.-Mexico border. [1]

The case is Mullin v. Refugee and Immigrant Center for Education and Legal Services. It grows out of a proclamation President Donald Trump signed on Jan. 20, 2025, declaring that "an invasion is ongoing at the southern border" and suspending the entry of noncitizens "engaged in the invasion." The Department of Homeland Security followed with guidance that barred those noncitizens from applying for asylum and sent anyone who crossed anyway into quick removal, without some of the procedures the law provides. [1]

Thirteen people who say they were persecuted or tortured in their home countries, joined by three immigration nonprofits, sued as a class. U.S. District Judge Randolph Moss threw out the DHS guidance and blocked officials from carrying out those parts of the proclamation. A divided panel of the D.C. Circuit largely agreed. Judge J. Michelle Childs, joined by Judge Cornelia Pillard, wrote that when Congress gave the president power to suspend entry by proclamation, it "did not intend to grant the Executive the expansive removal authority it asserts." Judge Justin Walker dissented in part, arguing the president can deny all asylum applications, but he agreed the government "cannot remove aliens to countries where they will be persecuted." The appeals court kept its ruling on hold, so the restrictions stay in force while the case goes on. [1]

The two sides frame it very differently. Solicitor General D. John Sauer told the justices the border "had become overwhelmed by an ungovernable influx of millions of aliens" and that the proclamation rests on "the President's inherent and delegated powers to exclude aliens." The challengers answered that the president "may not invent by fiat a new removal system that eviscerates the protections Congress mandated by statute," and that federal law says noncitizens "may apply" for asylum "irrespective of … status." They added that border apprehensions had already fallen more than 90 percent from their peak before the proclamation took effect. "If the Executive Branch nonetheless believes it needs new tools to manage the border," they wrote, "Congress must provide them." [1]

The Court agreed to decide whether the proclamation and guidance are lawful and whether Judge Moss had the power to grant relief that broad. It declined the government's separate request to review whether the class itself was properly formed. Arguments are likely next spring. [1] The case joins a crowded immigration docket that already includes third-country removals and detention without a hearing. [1]

**Commentary: the old map.** Strip away the border and the politics, and this is a boundary dispute between two branches. The Constitution hands Congress the power "To establish an uniform Rule of Naturalization" in Article I, and the asylum rules at issue are statutes Congress wrote. [2] The president, for his part, executes those laws and claims powers of his own over who may enter. The question for the justices is which statute wins when the two collide.

James Madison saw why the answer matters. "The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands," he wrote in Federalist No. 47, "may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny." [3] He was careful to add that the branches were never meant to be sealed off entirely, only that no one department should wield the whole power of another. [3] That is a fair lens for both sides here. If a crisis is real, the founders' answer is that the branch that makes the law can change it, quickly if it must. If Congress has already given the president the tools, the courts should say so plainly. What the framers would not have liked is a policy this large standing on a fight over which branch wrote the rule.
