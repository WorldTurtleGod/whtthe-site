Every giant starts somewhere. This one started enormous.

Scientists have described a new dinosaur species, Nyana rooiwalensis, from a near-complete skull and other bones collected in 2018 near Qhemegha village in South Africa's Eastern Cape. It lived in the Late Triassic, more than 200 million years ago, and it belonged to the sauropodomorphs — the lineage that later produced Brachiosaurus, Diplodocus and the largest land animals that ever lived. The describing paper, led by Atashni Moopen of the University of the Witwatersrand, was published in Royal Society Open Science. [1][2]

Here is the strange part. The bones say this animal was a youngster — at most two growth-arrest lines in its limb bones, a surface still showing rapid, active growth, an estimated age of at least its third year of life. And it already weighed an estimated 400 kilograms, about 880 pounds, walking probably on two legs. That is more than four times the size of juvenile Massospondylus or Mussaurus of comparable age — the known kids of its family and era. [1][2]

The name gives the game away: Nyana means "child" in isiXhosa and "small" or "little" in Sesotho. The child in question was pushing half a ton. "Extrapolation of these growth curves suggests that adult Nyana may have reached multi-tonne body masses," the authors write. [1][3]

The specimen is a landmark beyond its weight. Its preserved skull, 21.3 centimeters long — braincase, palate, hyoid and even the scleral rings of the eyes — is the first almost complete, articulated dinosaur skull from South Africa's Late Triassic, and one of only a handful from that time across the southern continents. Moopen's team used a synchrotron X-ray beam to build a detailed 3D model of it, and sliced limb bones to read its growth like tree rings. Family-tree analysis places Nyana as a close relative of either Sarahsaurus from North America or Ngwevu from South Africa. [1][2]

Why it matters: the great question about the sauropods is how a family of modest two-legged plant-eaters became forty-ton, four-legged giants. Nyana is evidence the size was there early — that this branch of the family was already supersizing its children in the Triassic, long before the famous giants walked. The giants did not invent bigness at the end of the story. At least one of them was born — well, hatched — already practicing.
